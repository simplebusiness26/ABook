# Voice Guide

## Target reading experience

The prose should feel effortless to read without feeling simplistic: vivid characters, clear scenes, sharp dialogue, warmth, dry humour and emotional weight when it matters.

The book should have an **original voice**. It can use broad qualities associated with accessible British commercial fiction—clean storytelling, playful observation, memorable supporting characters, escalating mystery and humour beside serious material—but it should not imitate the distinctive prose of any living author.

## Core rules

### 1. Readability first

- Prefer concrete language over elaborate description.
- Keep paragraphs relatively short during tense or comic scenes.
- Explain prison-specific ideas naturally through context.
- Avoid dumping terminology at the reader.
- Let Connor notice what a newcomer would genuinely notice.

### 2. Character before exposition

Instead of explaining a prison rule in narration, show somebody breaking it, fearing it, arguing about it or quietly teaching it.

### 3. Humour and seriousness can coexist

A chapter can contain tension and still have funny observations, awkward personalities or absurd institutional details. Humour should make the people feel real rather than trivialise consequences.

### 4. Every POV sounds different

Connor: observant, uncertain, mentally rehearsing what he should say, gradually becoming sharper.

Other prisoners: vocabulary, attention and assumptions should reflect their individual lives rather than one generic 'prison voice'.

Craig: because he has no POV in Book One, his dialogue should reveal just enough to make readers want access to his thoughts later.

### 5. End chapters with movement

Good endings include:
- a new piece of information;
- a decision;
- a changed relationship;
- a threat becoming clearer;
- a joke that exposes something serious;
- a question the next POV can partly answer.

Avoid artificial cliffhangers on every chapter.

## Multi-POV discipline

A POV change should act like turning the same object in your hand and discovering another side.

Example structure:
- Connor hears people whispering that 'Craig is here.'
- Another POV reveals why that matters.
- Connor later witnesses behaviour he still cannot fully interpret.
- A third POV reveals that an outside offer is influencing events.

This creates dramatic irony: the reader can understand more than Connor while still knowing less than Craig.

## Atmosphere

Important sensory details are ordinary and specific: doors, keys, waiting, televisions, meal routines, queues, paperwork, noise through walls, stale conversations, gym talk, phone calls, people watching who enters a room.

Use details to create a believable closed world, not to provide operational guidance for wrongdoing.

## Series identity

The recurring pleasure of the series should be: **recognition**.

A reader meets somebody briefly in Book One, then two books later sees their name as a chapter heading and thinks: *Oh, we're finally getting their story.*
