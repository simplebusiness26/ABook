# Voice Guide

## Target reading experience

The prose should feel effortless to read without feeling simplistic: vivid characters, clear scenes, sharp dialogue, warmth, dry humour and emotional weight when it matters.

The book should have an **original voice**. It can use broad qualities associated with accessible British commercial fiction—clean storytelling, playful observation, memorable supporting characters, escalating mystery and humour beside serious material—but it should not imitate the distinctive prose of any living author.

## Core rules

### 1. Readability first

- Prefer concrete language over elaborate description.
- Keep paragraphs relatively short during tense or comic scenes.
- Explain prison-specific ideas naturally through context.
- Avoid dumping terminology at the reader.
- Let Connor notice what a newcomer would genuinely notice.

### 2. Character before exposition

Instead of explaining a prison rule in narration, show somebody breaking it, fearing it, arguing about it or quietly teaching it.

### 3. Humour and seriousness can coexist

A chapter can contain tension and still have funny observations, awkward personalities or absurd institutional details. Humour should make the people feel real rather than trivialise consequences.

### 4. Every POV sounds different

Connor: observant, uncertain, mentally rehearsing what he should say, gradually becoming sharper.

Mason: experienced, dry, institutional, able to spot patterns and absurdity that newcomers miss.

Leah: practical, controlled, initially sceptical of prisoner mythology; notices behaviour and risk before personality.

Malik: hears networks, subtext and information value. He notices who knows what and who wants other people to know it.

Jay: socially alert, loyal to his own circle but sceptical of outside people volunteering prisoners for consequences they will never serve themselves.

Deano: territorial but pragmatic. He notices shifts in alliances and does not automatically inherit somebody else's feud.

Bennett: anxious, compartmentalising, always trying to convince himself that his private problem remains containable.

Bruce: fast, funny, sideways perspective. Humour should reveal pressure rather than turn him into a cartoon.

Other prisoners: vocabulary, attention and assumptions should reflect their individual lives rather than one generic 'prison voice'.

Craig: because he has no POV in Book One, his dialogue should reveal just enough to make readers want access to his thoughts later.

### 5. End chapters with movement

Good endings include:
- a new piece of information;
- a decision;
- a changed relationship;
- a threat becoming clearer;
- a joke that exposes something serious;
- a question the next POV can partly answer.

Avoid artificial cliffhangers on every chapter.

## Dated mosaic structure — LOCKED FOR BOOK ONE DRAFT V2

The main chapters are **dates**, not character names. The exact story year can remain unstated in the working draft until period-specific prison details are finalised.

Inside each dated chapter, use clearly labelled POV sections such as:

`## CONNOR`

`## MASON`

`## LEAH`

A dated chapter may contain only two viewpoints or may become deliberately busy with six, eight or more short sections. The rhythm should sometimes feel almost comic-book-like: one character receives a piece of information, cut; another interprets it differently, cut; somebody outside the prison reacts, cut; Connor is worrying about something completely ordinary while danger develops around him, cut.

### Mosaic rules

1. **Connor remains the anchor.** He receives the most page time and recurring sections. Readers should never lose the emotional centre of Book One.
2. **Craig receives no POV at all in Book One.** He can appear in everybody else's sections but the reader never enters his thoughts.
3. **Every switch is clearly labelled.** Never make the reader guess whose head they are in.
4. **Section length is elastic.** A POV can last several pages when emotion needs room, or only a few hundred words when the purpose is a quick hit of information or contrast.
5. **Cuts must earn themselves.** A switch should add information, alter interpretation, increase danger, create humour or provide emotional contrast.
6. **Busy does not mean confusing.** Keep chronology clear within each date and avoid introducing too many new names in a single paragraph.
7. **Use dramatic irony.** The reader should sometimes understand that danger is moving towards Connor or Craig before Connor understands it.
8. **Let information mutate.** Prison gossip should change slightly as it moves from Big Sharky to outside contacts to prisoners, cells, friendship groups and staff suspicion.
9. **Do not turn threat-building into operational instruction.** We can show that people are discussing an attack, money, pressure and loyalties without teaching practical methods of violence or evasion.
10. **Contrast large and small stakes.** A major prison-wide rumour can sit beside somebody worrying about a missed phone call, a child, a debt, a meal, a girlfriend or getting through the night.

### First-draft rhythm

The opening can remain heavily Connor-led so readers bond with him through arrest, custody, court and arrival. Once Connor reaches HMP Lewes, the frame opens outward and the book becomes increasingly mosaic-like.

A typical escalation can look like:

Connor arrives → Mason recognises the social meaning → Leah sees risk → word reaches Big Sharky → money is attached to Craig → Malik hears it → Jay's circle debates it → Deano's circle hears another version → Bennett notices pressure from a different angle → Connor remains mostly unaware → the threat closes in → Connor acts instinctively when it finally reaches Craig.

## Multi-POV discipline

A POV change should act like turning the same object in your hand and discovering another side.

Example structure:
- Connor hears people whispering that 'Craig is here.'
- Mason reveals what reputation looks like to an experienced prisoner.
- Leah sees the same arrival as a staff risk problem.
- Big Sharky hears the name hundreds of miles away and gives it a completely different meaning.
- Malik learns there is money attached.
- Connor later witnesses behaviour he still cannot fully interpret.

This creates dramatic irony: the reader can understand more than Connor while still knowing less than Craig.

## Atmosphere

Important sensory details are ordinary and specific: doors, keys, waiting, televisions, meal routines, queues, paperwork, noise through walls, stale conversations, gym talk, phone calls, people watching who enters a room.

Use details to create a believable closed world, not to provide operational guidance for wrongdoing.

## Series identity

The recurring pleasure of the series should be: **recognition**.

A reader meets somebody briefly in Book One, then two books later sees their name as a section heading or major viewpoint and thinks: *Oh, we're finally getting their story.*
