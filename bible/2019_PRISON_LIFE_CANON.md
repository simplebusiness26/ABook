# 2019 Prison-Life Canon

This file records the period-specific everyday prison life that should make **Hear All, See All, Say Nothing** feel lived-in rather than built only from major plot events.

## Story year — LOCKED

**Connor arrives at HMP Lewes in 2019.**

The exact opening date still needs to be reset. The existing October weekday/date placeholders were created before the year was locked and must not be treated as final calendar dates.

A major timing constraint now applies: if Book One runs beyond March 2020, the COVID-19 prison regime would become historically impossible to ignore. The preferred structure is therefore to keep the main Lewes story inside late 2019 / very early 2020 unless the creator deliberately chooses to make the pandemic part of the novel.

## Incentives / IEP status

Creator-supplied lived detail:

- prisoners talk about **IEP status**;
- the three levels relevant to the creator's remembered regime are **Basic, Standard and Enhanced**;
- Standard is the ordinary/default level in the remembered system;
- prisoners can lose privileges/status through poor behaviour, refusing regime expectations or repeated disciplinary problems;
- prisoners can work toward Enhanced through sustained good behaviour and engagement;
- Basic is not automatically socially embarrassing; many prisoners simply treat incentive status as another part of prison administration;
- some prisoners actively want Enhanced, some actively avoid Basic, and some do not care much either way;
- creator remembers Enhanced as bringing things prisoners genuinely value, including more gym opportunity, extra visits and a higher canteen spending allowance.

Official period note: in 2019 the old Incentives and Earned Privileges scheme was being replaced. Entry level ceased in August 2019 and Basic / Standard / Enhanced remained. Final manuscript terminology must be checked against the exact story month.

## Work and education

Creator remembers prisoners being expected, once settled beyond the introductory period, to engage with **work or education** rather than simply remain idle.

Common / useful story jobs:

- wing cleaner;
- laundry;
- kitchens;
- servery;
- reception orderly;
- induction orderly;
- gym orderly;
- segregation orderly;
- visits orderly.

The more trusted orderly jobs matter socially because they can mean more time off the wing, more routine, and a sense of progress.

Period reality should not be idealised: Lewes also had shortages of meaningful activity and unemployment. This is useful dramatically. Prisoners can be expected to engage while simultaneously waiting for allocations, losing jobs, complaining about unfair decisions, or being unable to get the activity they want.

## Character job canon

### Connor / Rush

Connor eventually becomes a **wing cleaner**.

This is partly a consequence of his prison reputation and repeated proximity to criminality. He wants more trusted work but learns that being seen as a security risk closes doors. His punishment is almost comic in its ordinariness: while imagining himself becoming important, he is still cleaning the landing, dealing with everybody else's mess and being chased by staff when the cleaning is not done properly.

This is an important theme: prison reputation can create status among prisoners while simultaneously reducing Connor's institutional opportunities.

### Jamo

Jamo has been doing **education** during his first six months and believes he is on the right track for a trusted **visits orderly** position.

He is genuinely excited by the possibility of being off the wing, busy and trusted. Around the same period that Connor becomes Rush, Jamo loses status / opportunity. That gives his later resentment of Connor a material foundation as well as jealousy.

### Mason

Mason is an **induction orderly**. This explains why he naturally crosses paths with new arrivals while living elsewhere in the prison.

### Leon

Leon is a **gym orderly**. The job fits his discipline/routine identity and gives him something concrete to lose when prison politics interfere with neutrality.

Other character jobs remain to be allocated during middle-structure planning.

## Canteen and ordinary prison economy

Canteen should be treated as a major part of everyday prison life.

Use scenes involving:

- waiting for canteen;
- mistakes or missing items;
- prisoners buying extra food;
- people informally cooking for others;
- a socially recognised "wing chef" who cooks better meals for friends or in exchange for ordinary favours/canteen value;
- arguments caused by shortages, mistakes or somebody believing they have been left out.

Do not turn these scenes into instructions for prohibited trade or contraband exchange.

## Cell searches, disciplinary paperwork and lost jobs

Cell searches ("spins") should appear naturally in the background of prison life.

They can lead to:

- prohibited property being found;
- prisoners being removed from jobs or trusted positions;
- disciplinary / adjudication paperwork;
- anxious waiting for paperwork or hearings;
- prisoners insisting an item was not theirs or arguing that staff have got the circumstances wrong;
- arguments over whether a decision is fair;
- incentive-status consequences.

The story should focus on uncertainty, frustration, institutional process and consequences. Do not include practical advice for hiding prohibited items, beating searches, exploiting paperwork mistakes or evading discipline.

Creator remembers the period after an incident but before paperwork arrives as emotionally significant: prisoners may spend the time hoping nothing further comes of it while already fearing the loss of a job or privileges.

## Spice / substance problem

Spice should exist as a serious and visible prison problem in the background, especially through Darren and Stuart's arcs.

Natural dialogue can include comments such as:

- Darren looks bad lately;
- people think Darren is heavily caught up in spice;
- a prisoner becomes confused, distressed or medically unwell and staff have to respond;
- a serious substance-related collapse can have lasting or even fatal consequences if later chosen for the plot.

Any depiction must remain non-graphic and non-instructional. Do not describe preparation, dosage, packaging, procurement, concealment or use technique.

## Ordinary recurring prison friction

The novel should repeatedly use small frustrations because this is what makes the prison feel real between major events:

- applications unanswered;
- canteen errors;
- job allocations and job losses;
- cancelled or disrupted visits;
- cellmate arguments;
- laundry problems;
- TV signal disappearing;
- random cell moves;
- cell searches;
- waiting for property or clothing parcels;
- gym-list disputes;
- people missing the gym list;
- servery arguments over meals or shortages;
- people complaining that staff decisions are inconsistent or unfair.

These moments should collide with the major plot. Somebody can be worrying about Craig while Macca is furious about a missed family call, Jamo is worried about losing a trusted job, Leon is arguing about gym access, and Darren is deteriorating for reasons that have nothing to do with Craig.

## Structural principle

The prison should feel like **different lives smashing together because they share one institution**.

One incident can cause:

- a lockdown that ruins Macca's call;
- Leon losing gym access;
- Jamo missing education or a job interview;
- Darren becoming more unstable;
- Malik hearing three incompatible versions of what happened;
- Connor treating the incident as a reputation problem;
- staff treating it as paperwork and risk management.

That contrast is central to the novel's identity.
