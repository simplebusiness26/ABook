# Series Bible

## Core promise

The series answers a deceptively simple question: **What is prison like?**

The answer is: it depends who you are.

Every recurring character experiences the same institution differently. Status, sentence, family, addiction, fear, reputation, money, age, personality and choices all change what prison becomes for them.

## Long-term engine

The series is designed as an expandable network rather than a closed trilogy.

Characters can:
- move between establishments;
- disappear for a book and return later;
- become leads after previously being side characters;
- cross paths years later under completely different circumstances;
- leave prison, fail or succeed outside, and return to the story naturally;
- anchor spin-offs in other prisons or periods.

Continuity matters. Every significant relationship, promise, betrayal, transfer and unresolved conflict should be tracked.

## Book One dramatic engine

Connor arrives as a first-time prisoner after becoming involved in crime and taking part in a robbery. He is not naturally suited to the criminal world and does not understand prison culture.

Craig arrives at roughly the same time. Unlike Connor, Craig's reputation reaches the wing before his personality does. Other prisoners know the name, talk about him and assess what his arrival means.

A serious financial incentive exists for somebody to attack Craig. Connor initially hears fragments of the story without understanding the full picture. During the early induction period, trouble finally reaches Craig. Connor intervenes on instinct and backs him.

That one choice changes Connor's position. He becomes increasingly associated with Craig while Craig awaits sentencing.

Craig remains an external mystery throughout Book One. The reader sees his effect on rooms, people and decisions, but never gets access to his private thoughts.

The end of Book One should create expansion rather than closure: Craig receives a major sentence and is moved on into the higher-security prison system, opening the door for Book Two to finally reveal his perspective and a much larger world.

## Point-of-view rules

- Each chapter has one clear POV character.
- Chapter titles use the POV character's name.
- No head-hopping inside scenes.
- Connor receives the most chapters in Book One.
- Craig receives zero POV chapters in Book One.
- POV switches must reveal something the previous character could not know.
- A new POV should earn its existence through a distinct life, problem or interpretation of events.

## Tone

The world can be threatening, sad, absurd, boring and funny within the same day.

Humour should mainly come from:
- personality clashes;
- institutional absurdity;
- banter;
- overconfidence;
- misunderstandings;
- recurring odd characters;
- ordinary problems becoming enormous inside a closed environment.

The story should never need to make dangerous behaviour instructional in order to feel authentic.

## Themes

Primary:
- prison is different for everyone;
- reputation versus reality;
- the cost of belonging;
- how one decision changes a life;
- identity inside institutions;
- loyalty, fear and self-preservation.

Secondary:
- family outside;
- boredom and routine;
- masculinity and vulnerability;
- addiction;
- exercise and self-discipline;
- money and status;
- humour as survival;
- hope, denial and reinvention.

## Recurring-world principle

No character should exist only as furniture. A cleaner, officer, gym obsessive, addict, quiet older prisoner or comic side character can later become central if the story discovers a strong enough life behind them.

The reader should gradually feel that every cell door hides another novel.
