# Series Bible

## Core promise

The series answers a deceptively simple question: **What is prison like?**

The answer is: it depends who you are.

Every recurring character experiences the same institution differently. Status, sentence, family, addiction, fear, reputation, money, age, race, neighbourhood, personality, staff role and personal choices all change what prison becomes for them.

## Long-term engine

The series is designed as an expandable network rather than a closed trilogy.

Characters can:
- move between establishments;
- disappear for a book and return later;
- become leads after previously being side characters;
- cross paths years later under completely different circumstances;
- leave prison, succeed or fail outside, and return to the story naturally;
- anchor spin-offs in other prisons or periods;
- remain in one prison while influencing events in another through relationships and outside contacts.

Continuity matters. Every significant relationship, promise, betrayal, transfer and unresolved conflict should be tracked.

## Book One location rule

Book One is overwhelmingly anchored in one fictional local/remand prison: **HMP Greybank**.

The point is that the reader can watch the same building become a completely different world depending on whose chapter they are reading.

Occasional chapters may leave Greybank when the story genuinely benefits from it. The main exception is **Big Sharky**, who is already held in a dispersal/high-security prison and can influence Greybank from a distance.

## Book One dramatic engine

Connor arrives at Greybank as a first-time prisoner after becoming involved in crime and taking part in a robbery. He is not naturally suited to the criminal world and does not understand prison culture.

Craig arrives at roughly the same time. Unlike Connor, Craig's reputation reaches the wing before his personality does. Prisoners and staff react to his name before Connor understands why.

Far away, Big Sharky hears that Craig has landed at Greybank. Their history is deliberately withheld. Through intermediaries, pressure reaches the prison and a serious financial incentive is attached to somebody attacking Craig.

Connor initially hears fragments without understanding the full picture. During induction, trouble eventually reaches Craig. Connor intervenes on instinct and backs him.

That one choice changes Connor's position. He becomes increasingly associated with Craig while Craig awaits sentencing.

At the same time, the same central events are experienced through entirely different lives:
- Malik sees information becoming dangerous currency;
- Jay and Deano see social groups being pulled into a conflict they did not create;
- Darren experiences the effect on debt and addiction;
- Leon tries to preserve neutrality;
- Mason recognises the cycle of prison identity;
- Officer Leah Mercer becomes emotionally attached to Craig despite professional boundaries;
- Officer Tom Bennett becomes trapped in a corruption/coercion storyline involving pressure to bring contraband packages into the prison;
- staff see risk, paperwork and consequences where prisoners see loyalty, reputation and legend.

Craig remains an external mystery throughout Book One. The reader sees his effect on rooms, people and decisions but never gets access to his private thoughts.

The end of Book One creates expansion rather than closure: Craig receives a major sentence and is transferred into the dispersal/high-security system.

Book Two opens the door to Craig's POV and eventually puts him in the same world as Big Sharky, where the reason for their feud can finally be revealed.

## Point-of-view rules

- Each chapter has one clear POV character.
- Chapter titles use the POV character's name.
- No head-hopping inside scenes.
- Connor receives the largest share of Book One chapters.
- Craig receives **zero POV chapters in Book One**.
- POV switches must reveal something the previous character could not know.
- Not every recurring character needs a POV chapter.
- A new POV should earn its existence through a distinct life, problem or interpretation of events.
- Big Sharky's rare Book One interludes may show reach and intent, but never explain the origin of his feud with Craig.

## Social-world rule

Greybank has visible social clusters. Race can be one factor, as can neighbourhood, outside friendships, family links, religion, age and criminal associations.

Some crews may be predominantly Black or predominantly white, but the novel should never write either race as a single bloc. Individuals cross groups constantly through friendship, work, sport, cells, humour and mutual interest.

The goal is authenticity through specific people, not racial shorthand.

## Officer-story rule

Officers are not background furniture. They have their own pressures, friendships, fears, ambitions and blind spots.

Officer Leah Mercer's attachment to Craig should be emotionally believable while preserving the seriousness of the officer/prisoner power boundary.

Officer Tom Bennett's corruption plot should focus on coercion, compromise and consequences. We do not need operational detail about how contraband is physically smuggled in for the storyline to feel real.

## Tone

The world can be threatening, sad, absurd, boring and funny within the same day.

Humour should mainly come from:
- personality clashes;
- institutional absurdity;
- banter;
- overconfidence;
- misunderstandings;
- recurring odd characters;
- ordinary problems becoming enormous inside a closed environment.

Bruce is an important recurring pressure-release character, but serious harm is never the punchline.

## Themes

Primary:
- prison is different for everyone;
- reputation versus reality;
- the cost of belonging;
- how one decision changes a life;
- identity inside institutions;
- loyalty, fear and self-preservation.

Secondary:
- family outside;
- boredom and routine;
- masculinity and vulnerability;
- addiction;
- exercise and self-discipline;
- money and status;
- race, friendship and group identity;
- staff corruption and institutional pressure;
- professional boundaries;
- humour as survival;
- hope, denial and reinvention.

## Recurring-world principle

No character should exist only as furniture. A cleaner, officer, gym obsessive, addict, quiet older prisoner, healthcare worker or comic side character can later become central if the story discovers a strong enough life behind them.

The reader should gradually feel that every cell door — and every staff-room door — hides another novel.
