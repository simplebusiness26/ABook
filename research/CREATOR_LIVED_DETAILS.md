# Creator-Supplied Lived Detail Notes

These notes record story-world and prison-life details supplied directly by the creator. They are **not automatically treated as publicly verified facts** about HMP Lewes. Before publication, period-specific operational details should be checked where possible and fictionalised where appropriate.

## Connor background

- Connor is **21** when he is arrested.
- He comes from a rough, single-parent household where money has been tight.
- A major reason Connor tells himself he got into crime is to help support his family and make sure his brothers and siblings can have things they otherwise could not afford.
- He has a mum, brothers, a sister and a particularly young little brother.
- His strongest homesickness in prison is for his mum and siblings, especially his little brother.
- Connor has a girlfriend when he enters custody.
- During his imprisonment his girlfriend cheats on him. This should become an emotional subplot about the outside world continuing without him, rather than Connor's entire identity being reduced to the relationship.

## Connor opening sequence

- Connor is already involved in crime before prison and runs a cannabis line using runners.
- Opening night: Connor is at home with several runners/friends, relaxing as the night winds down after spending the day working his line.
- **Critical continuity rule:** before the armed police arrive, Connor does **not** believe he has an active burglary case and does not have a solicitor dealing with it. He believes he got away with the offence because time has passed and nothing happened.
- The aggravated burglary can exist only as an occasional buried memory before the raid. It is not what he is consciously worrying about while sitting at home.
- He finishes the last of a joint and kicks his shoes off immediately before armed police arrive.
- In panic, Connor throws the cannabis and phone associated with the line from the window. This is written as panic and loss of control, never as guidance on avoiding evidence.
- The drug material does not become part of the criminal case he ultimately faces; the aggravated burglary is the case that puts him on remand.
- The arrest is the trigger that brings the old offence crashing back into Connor's mind and tells him what the police have actually come for.
- Only after arrest/custody does a solicitor become part of Connor's burglary case.

## Critical arrest-to-prison timeline

- **Do not compress Connor's arrest, police custody, court and arrival at HMP Lewes into one day.** The procedure itself should occupy multiple days and the waiting is part of the emotional story.
- Working chronology: Connor is arrested **late on a Friday**.
- Friday night: armed arrest, transport into police custody, first shock and initial cell time.
- Saturday: police custody continues; solicitor consultation, blood-evidence revelation, interview and the allegation about a firearm all have room to breathe rather than happening as a rapid montage.
- Sunday: more waiting, uncertainty, cells, interrupted sleep, family thoughts and the psychological strain of not knowing what comes next. Exact procedural details can be adjusted after period-specific research, but Connor is **not yet in HMP Lewes**.
- Monday: Connor reaches court, is remanded, experiences the decisive realisation that he is not going home, then travels in prison transport and arrives at **HMP Lewes**.
- The key storytelling principle is duration: by the time Connor reaches Lewes he has already had several days in which his ordinary life has disappeared and he has had almost nothing to do except think.
- The precise weekday/date/year can be locked later, but all future drafts must preserve the multi-day progression.

## Aggravated burglary facts in the story

- The offence is an **aggravated burglary**.
- Watches are stolen.
- A Mercedes C63 AMG belonging to the victim is also taken.
- The Mercedes is later recovered.
- Connor accidentally cuts the **middle finger** of one hand during the offence.
- He notices the cut afterwards but does not connect it with the burglary and does not realise he has left blood in the recovered car.
- There is blood evidence in/on the recovered car linking Connor to the offence.
- Because no arrest comes immediately, he gradually convinces himself he got away with it.
- After his arrest, his solicitor tells him that the recovered car contains blood attributed to Connor. This is the moment Connor suddenly thinks, essentially, *shit — that must have been when*.
- Connor is the **only participant caught** because the physical evidence identifies him but does not identify the other people involved.
- Connor gives a **no-comment interview** and does not identify his co-offenders.
- Witnesses report that a firearm was present during the burglary. Police never recover one and Connor does not admit possessing or using one.
- Detectives repeatedly frame the alleged firearm as the point at which Connor “crossed the line” and as a major reason the case is treated so seriously.
- The novel must distinguish allegation from fact: witnesses say they saw a gun; police believe one was present; none is recovered; Connor does not admit it.
- We do not need operational detail about the weapon.
- The solicitor can describe the blood evidence bluntly as Connor being effectively **“caught red-handed.”**
- Connor's emotional reaction matters more than forensic procedure: an insignificant cut he had almost forgotten becomes the thing that changes the next several years of his life.

## Police custody / court emotion

- Connor spends time in a holding cell replaying what happened and, now that he knows why he was arrested, obsessively thinking about the aggravated burglary.
- The emotional contrast matters: immediately before the raid the offence was barely in his conscious mind; immediately afterwards it becomes almost impossible for him to think about anything else.
- When the solicitor explains the blood evidence in the recovered Mercedes, Connor remembers the cut on his middle finger and finally connects the two events.
- That revelation should land before the interview and make Connor feel that the case has suddenly become real and difficult to explain away.
- Interview should feel psychologically intimidating from Connor's perspective.
- Connor answers **“no comment”** rather than giving an account and refuses to identify the others involved.
- The detective uses the witness allegation of a firearm to emphasise the seriousness of the case and tells Connor that he has crossed a line.
- Connor hears frightening sentence estimates and possibilities from different people and fixates on the worst numbers.
- Court ends with Connor being remanded rather than released.
- The emotional point is the moment he understands he is not going home.

## Prison transport

- On the journey to HMP Lewes Connor passes parts of the area/world he knows outside.
- Seeing normal life through the prison transport is emotionally important.
- His thoughts should include his mum, siblings, girlfriend and especially his little brother, alongside obsessive thoughts about sentence length.
- He convinces himself he could receive around fifteen years.
- Later in the story Connor ultimately receives a five-year sentence, but during remand he has no way to know the outcome will be better than his worst fears.
- Theme: at the start of a sentence/remand period, uncertainty can make the imagined future much worse than the eventual outcome.

## Craig — creator-supplied core concept

- Craig is originally from London but becomes a dominant organised-crime figure across Sussex.
- He is known for money, connections, loyalty, fearlessness and a reputation for violence.
- He is intended to feel like the **boss above the bosses** rather than an ordinary local gang leader.
- Connor's old drug line turns out to sit several layers beneath Craig's wider network. Connor had been selling product that ultimately came through Craig's organisation without knowing who Craig was.
- Craig likes Connor because he sees something of his younger self in him.
- Working legal realism: Craig's headline allegation should be a **large-scale conspiracy to supply Class A drugs**, with the prosecution presenting him as the leading organiser. Supporting counts can include participating in an organised crime group and money-laundering offences.
- The story can call this “the conspiracy” rather than repeatedly dumping legal language into dialogue.

## Craig / Big Sharky — hidden history

- Big Sharky is older than Craig.
- Craig and Big Sharky effectively grew up around one another, with Big Sharky originally occupying the senior position.
- Big Sharky later went to prison.
- He came to believe that Craig had set him up or otherwise done him dirty.
- That belief is wrong or based on an incomplete understanding of what really happened.
- Craig's rise while Big Sharky has been imprisoned deepens the resentment.
- **Book One must not explain this.** It should only hint that their feud is old and personal.
- The full truth is reserved for Book Two and can be refined later once the creator develops the incident in more depth.

## Arrival / induction atmosphere

- Connor's arrival should be emotionally daunting, not skipped over as procedure.
- There is an imposing female officer involved in induction/reception who immediately lays down expectations because Connor is young.
- Her manner repeatedly demands his attention: essentially, “Look at me when I'm talking to you.”
- Two prisoner peer-workers/orderlies are present nearby. To Connor they look extremely large, hardened and intimidating; he interprets them as men who may have served very long sentences/life sentences.
- The point is Connor's perception: fear makes experienced prisoners look almost superhuman to a first-timer.
- After this induction stage Connor reaches the reception holding/cell area.
- **This is where Connor first sees Craig.**
- Craig should not have been visible earlier in the chapter.

## Prison advice Connor receives

- At a moment when Connor is becoming too curious about the conversations and politics around Craig, an experienced prisoner gives him a piece of advice the creator remembers receiving on first coming to prison:
  **“Hear all. See all. Say nothing.”**
- Meaning in the story: notice what is happening around you, listen, learn, but do not feel compelled to repeat every piece of information or insert yourself into every situation.
- This should become a memorable recurring line for Connor rather than a one-off slogan. Later choices can be measured against whether he follows or ignores it.
- Mason is a strong fit to deliver it because he already functions as Connor's experienced long-view warning voice.

## Writing principle extracted from these details

The series must show the **emotion of process**. Arrest, interview, court, transport, induction, waiting, cell doors and uncertainty are not filler between plot events. For a first-time prisoner, they *are* major events.

A reader should understand not only what happened to Connor but what his mind does to every stage of it.
