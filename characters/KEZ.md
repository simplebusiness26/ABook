# Kez — Book One Character Canon

## Core role

Kez is not a major kingpin and should never compete with Craig for narrative gravity.

He is a **middle-level prison operator**: experienced enough to know how the wing works, socially connected enough to make things happen through other people, and confident enough to make younger/newer prisoners feel that being useful to him is the same as being welcomed into something important.

His strength is not physical dominance. It is social judgement.

He notices what somebody wants to believe about themselves and talks to that version of them.

With Connor, he sees immediately that the new nickname **Rush** has created a vulnerability:

- Connor wants the reputation to be real;
- Connor wants money;
- Connor wants older/established prisoners to see him as reliable;
- Connor does not want to look frightened after the Craig incident made him look brave.

Kez does not need to make an explicit threat. He flatters Connor into volunteering.

## Age / presence

Working age: **late twenties**.

Kez has been inside before and understands prison socially better than Connor does.

He should look ordinary enough that his influence is initially easy to underestimate. He is not huge, not a legendary fighter and not someone the whole jail fears.

He is nevertheless **not a harmless victim**. He has friends, associates and outside connections, and he is used to disputes carrying consequences beyond one conversation.

## Personality

- relaxed when someone else is carrying the immediate risk;
- charming when he wants something;
- good at making exploitation sound like opportunity;
- rarely explains more than he needs to;
- dislikes being challenged publicly;
- not cowardly, but prefers leverage and people to unnecessary direct confrontation;
- believes Connor should have asked questions if Connor wanted a better deal;
- capable of giving a little ground when that is easier than creating a bigger dispute.

That last point matters. Kez is not written as a cartoon thief who pockets everything. He has genuine costs and other people attached to the arrangement. But he also deliberately leaves Connor ignorant of the split because that ignorance benefits Kez.

## The visit storyline

Kez first approaches Connor on **2 October 2019**.

He gases Connor up with ideas of:

- making money;
- building a proper name;
- proving he is reliable;
- showing that Rush is more than a nickname.

Kez does **not** tell Connor the eventual financial split at this point, and Connor does not ask.

The visitor then has to be added to Connor's approved visiting arrangements. This creates a deliberate wait of more than two weeks rather than an implausibly immediate visit.

The dangerous visit occurs on **19 October 2019**.

Only after Connor returns does Kez reveal that Connor's initial share is roughly **one fifth**.

Connor becomes furious and feels like a mug because he carried the immediate risk without knowing the terms.

When Connor nearly loses his temper, Kez gives a little ground and raises Connor's share to roughly **one quarter**. Kez argues that not everything he retains is profit because he has to cover the cost of the pack and other costs attached to the arrangement.

The important dramatic point is that **both things can be true**:

- Kez may have genuine costs;
- Kez still saw Connor coming and let him take the risk without telling him the terms.

Craig later explains exactly that distinction to Connor. Craig does not simply side with Connor. He points out that the unfairness is not only the percentage; it is that Connor agreed first and learned the value of his own risk afterwards.

The story should not explain practical smuggling or concealment mechanics.

## Could Connor beat Kez in a fight?

**Yes, plausibly.**

Connor is younger, more explosive and physically confident after the Craig incident. Kez is not designed as a feared fighter. In a straightforward one-on-one confrontation, it is believable that Connor could get the better of him.

But that is deliberately **not the same question as whether beating Kez up is a good move**.

Kez's protection is social rather than physical.

If Connor attacks him, likely story consequences include:

- staff intervention and disciplinary consequences for Connor;
- possible segregation / loss of privileges / additional security attention;
- further damage to Connor's chances of trusted work;
- Jamo becoming even more convinced that Connor's problems are entering their shared cell;
- Kez's associates treating the assault as a wider dispute rather than a private argument;
- Connor gaining some fear/respect while simultaneously looking easier to manipulate emotionally;
- Craig being unimpressed that Kez managed to exploit Connor **and then make Connor lose his head afterwards**.

The exact retaliation, if any, should remain a story decision and should not be written as operational planning.

## Best current dramatic use

The strongest current version is that Connor **nearly** attacks Kez immediately after learning the split but stops.

Kez notices how close Connor is to losing his temper and concedes a little, moving Connor from roughly a fifth to a quarter.

That does four useful things:

1. It keeps the anger real.
2. It makes Kez feel like a believable negotiator rather than a one-note exploiter.
3. It allows Connor to recognise the trap a fraction earlier than he would have at the start of the book.
4. It gives Craig's lesson more weight: genuine costs can exist **and** Connor can still have been used.

A later physical confrontation remains available if the wider story earns it.

## Story purpose

Kez teaches Connor his first practical lesson about status without being Connor's mentor.

Craig teaches through observation and judgement.

Kez teaches by exploiting the exact insecurity Connor has not yet admitted he has.

The lesson is not 'be better at crime.'

It is:

**flattery can make a person volunteer for consequences that belong to somebody else.**
