# Darren and His Mate — Book One Arc

## Canon decision

**Darren does not die in Book One.**

His addiction/informing storyline remains important, but the fatal consequence falls on a younger/newer prisoner Darren takes under his wing.

The younger prisoner's name is **not yet locked**.

## Why this works

Darren should not exist simply as "the spice addict." He needs relationships, humour, loyalty, shame and responsibility.

Giving him a younger mate creates a mirror of several other relationships in the book:

- Craig sees something of himself in Connor.
- Mason tries to guide Connor away from mistakes.
- Darren also becomes a mentor — but he passes on a damaged version of prison survival because he is still trapped in his own dependency.

The tragedy is not that Darren deliberately harms the younger man. Darren genuinely likes him and thinks he is helping him settle in.

But Darren introduces him to Darren's world: the people he spends time with, the normalisation of substance use around him, the excuses, the debts and the idea that getting through the day matters more than thinking about next month.

## The younger mate

Working characteristics:

- younger/newer than Darren;
- arrives relatively inexperienced with prison;
- initially sees Darren as funny, streetwise and helpful;
- Darren likes having somebody look up to him;
- their friendship should have ordinary warmth: food, jokes, wing gossip, helping each other through boring days;
- the younger man gradually begins copying Darren's habits and spending more time around the same substance-using circle.

Do not make him a disposable victim who exists only to die. The reader needs to like him first.

## Progression

### 1. Darren as the experienced one

The younger prisoner is fresh enough that Darren suddenly gets to be somebody else's Jamo/Mason figure.

That feels good to Darren. For once he is not the man everybody looks at and says, "Darren looks rough." He is the one who knows the wing.

### 2. Normalisation

The younger mate begins spending more time with Darren and the people around him.

The book should show the change socially and emotionally rather than explaining drug-use mechanics. Other prisoners notice he looks different, misses things he previously cared about, or is becoming harder to reach.

### 3. Darren notices but minimises it

Darren should have opportunities to recognise what is happening.

He tells himself things like:

- the lad is an adult;
- everybody makes their own choices;
- he is only showing him how prison works;
- things are not that bad;
- he can sort himself out later.

Those excuses matter because Darren already uses similar excuses about himself.

### 4. Fatal substance-related collapse

Later in Book One, the younger mate suffers a fatal substance-related collapse.

Keep the scene **non-graphic and non-instructional**. The emotional focus is:

- sudden alarm / staff response;
- prisoners realising this is more serious than the usual chaos;
- the wing going strangely quiet afterwards;
- rumours spreading before facts are known;
- Darren understanding who it is;
- Darren being left alive with the knowledge that he helped normalise the world the younger man entered.

Do not describe dosage, preparation, procurement, concealment or detailed physical symptoms.

## Darren's consequence

This should not instantly cure Darren.

That would be too easy and would weaken the addiction story.

Instead it creates a wound he cannot comfortably explain away.

Darren may still relapse. He may still inform for Grant. He may still lie to himself.

But from this point onward, his addiction has a face attached to it.

When people tell Darren he is ruining himself, he can ignore them.

When he remembers the younger man who followed him into the same world and did not survive it, that is harder.

## Relationship to Grant / informing arc

The death should complicate Darren's relationship with Officer Grant.

Grant may see Darren as even more vulnerable and therefore easier to pressure for information.

Darren, meanwhile, may become more resentful of everybody who treats vulnerable prisoners as useful pieces — prisoners and staff alike.

This creates the possibility of a later decision where Darren finally refuses to give Grant something important, not because Darren has become morally pure, but because he has reached the limit of what he can live with.

## Ensemble collision possibilities

The death should ripple through other characters without becoming everybody's story:

- Connor hears about it while preoccupied with his own sentencing/status and briefly sees how small his reputation games can look beside an actual death.
- Stuart's deterioration becomes more frightening in retrospect.
- Malik hears multiple versions of what happened and refuses to spread one of the uglier rumours.
- Macca thinks about the dead prisoner's family receiving the call.
- Leon sees another example of why he tries to keep his life controlled.
- Jamo remembers when the lad first arrived and looked normal.
- Grant treats the aftermath institutionally, which can make Darren hate him more.
- Leah/Bennett see the staff-side consequence and paperwork while prisoners experience grief, guilt and gossip.

## Darren's Book One endpoint

Darren survives Book One.

He should end it changed but unresolved: still vulnerable, still capable of relapse, but no longer able to pretend that his choices only affect himself.

That keeps Darren alive for later books and gives him a much stronger long-term arc than killing him for shock value.
