# 16 OCTOBER

## BIG SHARKY

The news reached Big Sharky while he was eating toast.

That annoyed him.

Not the news.

The timing.

There were messages that deserved a room, a chair, perhaps a minute of silence beforehand.

This one arrived between butter and marmalade.

He read it once.

Then again.

**Craig's at Lewes. Confirmed.**

Big Sharky put the toast down.

The man opposite him kept talking for another few seconds before noticing he had lost his audience.

"What?"

Big Sharky looked up.

"Nothing."

The man knew him well enough not to ask twice.

Craig.

There had been years when the name was part of every day.

Years when it meant a boy from London who followed older lads around and learned faster than people realised.

Then years when Big Sharky had heard it from a distance.

Craig's doing well.

Craig's moved down Sussex way.

Craig's got people.

Craig's got money.

Craig's got half the coast calling him first.

Every report came with an extra floor added to the building.

Big Sharky had been inside for much of the construction.

That had its own taste.

He picked up the message again.

Lewes.

Close enough for the world to feel small.

The man opposite him finally gave in.

"Bad news?"

Big Sharky put the phone away.

"Old news."

"Then why you looking like that?"

Big Sharky smiled.

"Like what?"

The man suddenly became interested in his tea.

Big Sharky stood.

He did not make speeches. Speeches were for men who needed witnesses to believe they had made a decision.

He made one call later.

A short one.

He did not explain history.

He did not say what Craig had supposedly done years ago.

He did not say that every success attached to Craig's name had, in Big Sharky's mind, been built partly on something stolen from him.

He simply made his position known.

Craig was in Lewes.

There was money for the person who dealt with the problem.

Then he ended the call and went back to the rest of his day.

That was the part people misunderstood about grudges.

They imagined rage.

Sometimes rage lasted ten minutes.

A proper grudge could have breakfast.

## JAY

Jay heard about the money from a man who had heard about it from another man who insisted he had heard it directly.

This meant, in prison terms, that nobody knew anything.

"How much?" Leon asked.

"Depends which version."

They were sitting together after lunch, four men talking about five different things at once.

One conversation concerned football.

One concerned a missing charger.

One concerned Craig.

The Craig one kept winning.

"I'm hearing serious," said Rico.

Jay looked at him.

"Serious isn't a number."

"Enough."

"Enough for what?"

Rico shrugged.

That irritated Jay more than an answer would have.

Outside people loved prison mathematics.

They attached numbers to things and imagined the number did all the work.

Five grand. Ten grand. Twenty.

Whatever the figure was, the person saying it outside would go home afterwards. Put the kettle on. Sleep in his own bed. Check his phone in the morning.

The person inside would still be inside.

Jay had learned that lesson young.

"Who put it up?" Leon asked.

Rico lowered his voice.

"Sharky."

Jay did not react immediately.

That name carried weight too.

Different weight.

Older.

Further away.

The kind built from men saying, *before your time*.

"Big Sharky?" Leon said.

Rico nodded.

"That's what I'm hearing."

Jay leaned back.

"Then whoever's hearing it can do it themselves."

Rico laughed.

"You think anyone's asking you?"

"I'm making sure they don't."

"You lot know people on his side, though."

There it was.

Not a request.

The little bridge before a request.

Jay looked at Rico properly.

"Everybody knows somebody. Doesn't mean everybody works for everybody."

"I'm just saying."

"Then stop just saying."

The table went quiet for half a beat.

Then Leon said, "United are still finishing sixth."

The football argument restarted with almost grateful speed.

Jay let it.

But the Craig conversation had not gone anywhere.

It had simply moved behind the others.

That was how prison pressure worked most of the time.

Not a man standing over you making demands.

A sentence somebody dropped casually.

A name.

A reminder of who knew your cousin.

A suggestion that people outside expected you to see things the same way they did.

Jay had people outside.

Family.

Friends.

Old loyalties.

He also had years of his own life to serve.

He was not spending them like loose change because somebody with a grudge wanted to make a point.

At least, that was what he told himself.

## CONNOR

Connor had discovered that prison gave him too much time and never enough time at exactly the same moment.

Hours dragged.

Then suddenly somebody told him he had two minutes to be somewhere he did not understand.

He spent the morning trying to work out a form.

The form was winning.

"What does this bit mean?" he asked Mason.

Mason glanced at it.

"Means you put your address."

"I know what address means. This bit."

"Previous address."

"It's the same address."

"Then write it again."

Connor stared at the paper.

"Why?"

Mason shrugged.

"Prison likes proof you can repeat yourself."

Connor wrote it again.

Three seats away Craig was reading something.

Connor had become aware of him without wanting to be aware of him.

That was different from staring.

Probably.

Craig looked up.

"You filling in your autobiography?"

"Feels like it."

"Wait till they ask the same thing tomorrow."

Connor looked at Mason.

Mason nodded solemnly.

"And Friday."

"You're both taking the piss."

"Bit early for that," Craig said.

Connor smiled despite himself.

It was strange how quickly somebody could become familiar when everything else was unfamiliar.

He still did not know Craig.

Not really.

He knew the name made people look.

He knew the word *conspiracy* followed him around like a second surname.

He knew Mason thought Connor should stay away from other people's problems.

He also knew Craig had asked about his little brother without asking enough to make Connor defensive.

That counted for something.

"What you in for?" Craig asked.

Connor hesitated.

It was the first question everybody seemed to ask and the one Connor least wanted to answer.

"Burglary."

Craig waited.

Connor hated people who waited. Waiting made you fill silence.

"Aggravated."

Craig's expression changed almost invisibly.

"First time?"

"Yeah."

"How old?"

"Twenty-one."

Craig nodded.

Connor waited for advice.

Adults loved advice when they found out you were twenty-one.

Craig went back to reading.

"That's it?" Connor said.

"What's what?"

"You ain't gonna tell me I've ruined my life?"

Craig looked up again.

"Have you?"

Connor opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

Craig returned to the page.

"Thought so."

Connor looked at the form.

For the rest of the morning, the question irritated him because he could not answer it.

## BENNETT

Tom Bennett deleted the message and immediately regretted deleting it.

Then regretted regretting it.

He stood in the staff changing area staring at his phone as if the message might reappear through guilt.

It had contained six words.

Nothing illegal in themselves.

Nothing threatening if shown to someone who did not know the previous twenty messages.

That was the clever part.

People imagined threats arrived with threats in them.

Sometimes they arrived as reminders.

*Hope your dad's doing better mate.*

Bennett's dad was not doing better.

That was why this had started.

Money.

Debt.

One stupid favour asked by somebody who knew somebody.

A problem Bennett had thought lived entirely outside the prison.

Then it had learned his work address.

He locked his phone away and went on shift.

Leah saw him almost immediately.

"You look rough."

"Thanks."

"Compliment."

"Need to work on those."

She kept looking.

Bennett smiled.

"Didn't sleep."

"Kids?"

He nearly said yes even though he did not have children.

That frightened him more than the message.

Lies were starting to arrive before questions finished.

"Dad," he said instead.

Leah's expression softened.

"Sorry."

"It's fine."

It wasn't.

Across the landing somebody shouted Craig's name in a joke Bennett did not catch.

Another officer said something about everybody suddenly becoming an organised-crime expert.

Bennett laughed when the others laughed.

Part of him wondered if the men pressuring him knew about Craig.

Then he hated himself for even connecting the two things.

Every problem inside prison eventually tried to become the same problem if you let it.

Bennett told himself he would not.

He would sort his father.

Sort the money.

Sort the favour.

Draw a line.

Go back to normal.

It was a good plan.

The sort of plan people made immediately before discovering normal had already left.

## MALIK

By four in the afternoon, the rumour had a number.

By five, it had three.

Malik heard five thousand from one person, ten from another and a figure so stupid from a third that he laughed in the man's face.

"I'm telling you."

"You're telling me rubbish."

"My boy knows."

"Your boy also told everyone he was getting released last Christmas."

"Different thing."

"Same boy."

The man walked off annoyed.

Malik stopped smiling as soon as he was gone.

The number did not matter yet.

The source did.

Big Sharky.

That name had now arrived from two directions.

Independent directions.

That changed things.

Rumours were smoke.

Two unrelated people telling you where the fire was deserved attention.

Malik found Jay later.

"You heard?"

Jay did not look up.

"Depends."

"Sharky."

Jay's eyes lifted.

That was enough.

"Yeah," Jay said.

"Real?"

"Real enough that people are talking like idiots."

Malik sat beside him.

"How much?"

Jay gave him a flat look.

"See? Idiot."

Malik smiled.

"Occupational hazard."

Jay leaned closer, voice low.

"Don't get yourself stuck in the middle of that."

"I'm not in anything."

"You know too much about things you're not in."

"That's why people like me."

"That's why people eventually stop liking you."

Malik's smile stayed in place.

The words landed anyway.

Across the room Craig was talking to Connor.

That looked wrong somehow.

Not because Connor was anybody.

Because Connor was nobody.

Two days ago the lad had not existed in Lewes.

Now he was standing beside the most talked-about man in the prison, laughing at something.

Malik watched for a second.

"Who's the kid?" he asked.

Jay followed his gaze.

"New boy."

"He with Craig?"

"He arrived with a pulse and a bad sense of where to stand. That's about all I know."

Malik looked away.

There were moments when a person became part of a story before anybody, including the person, realised it.

Connor looked like he was having one.
