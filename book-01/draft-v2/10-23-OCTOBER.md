# 23 OCTOBER

## CONNOR

The conversation that changed Connor's understanding of Craig began with a packet of biscuits.

Nothing important in prison seemed to begin importantly.

Connor had two biscuits left.

Craig had none.

"One," Connor said.

Craig held out his hand.

"Two."

"There's two in the packet."

"Exactly."

"That's robbery."

Craig looked at him.

"Bit insensitive considering your case."

Connor laughed and threw him one.

"Greedy bastard."

Craig opened it.

"Character building."

They sat in easy silence for a while.

Connor had stopped finding silence around Craig awkward. That felt like an achievement.

A week ago every unanswered question had made him fill the gap.

Now he could wait.

Mostly.

"Malik was asking about my line yesterday," Connor said.

Craig looked over.

"Was he?"

"Nosy prick."

"That's his job."

"Who gave him the job?"

"Himself. Best promotions are self-appointed in here."

Connor smiled.

"He asked who I got through."

Craig's expression stayed casual.

"And?"

"Told him nothing."

"Good."

Connor glanced at him.

"Why good?"

"Because not everybody needs every bit of your life."

"You sound like Mason."

"That's hurtful."

Connor laughed.

Then, because he was Connor and curiosity had survived every warning available to it, he added, "Mikey."

Craig stopped chewing.

Only for a second.

Connor noticed.

"What?"

Craig looked at him.

"Mikey who?"

Connor gave the surname.

Craig leaned back.

The amusement had gone from his face, replaced by something Connor had seen only in flashes.

Calculation.

"What?" Connor said again.

"Nothing."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing everyone in here does where they say nothing and mean a whole fucking paragraph."

Craig smiled.

"Mikey wasn't your top man."

Connor frowned.

"Never said he was."

"You thought he was bigger than he was."

"How do you know Mikey?"

Craig looked down at the biscuit in his hand.

"I know who Mikey knew."

Connor stared.

"What does that mean?"

"Means the world is usually taller than it looks from the bottom."

Connor felt a strange chill.

Outside, his line had felt like his business.

His phone.

His runners.

His money.

His decisions.

He had known there were people above Mikey. Obviously. Everybody knew there was always somebody above somebody.

But those people had been abstract.

Not people.

Not men sitting beside him eating stolen biscuits.

"Was my stuff yours?" Connor asked.

Craig did not answer immediately.

That was an answer.

Connor sat forward.

"No fucking way."

Craig smiled slightly.

"Not directly."

"But—"

"Several layers."

Connor stared at him.

The wing seemed to get larger and smaller at the same time.

"So I was working for you?"

"You were working for yourself."

"But the line—"

"Sat inside a bigger world you couldn't see."

Connor laughed once because the alternative was simply staring.

"This is mad."

Craig shrugged.

"It's normal. You just didn't know it was normal."

Connor thought about all the people outside who acted important because three lads answered their phone.

Thought about Mikey.

Thought about himself.

Then looked at Craig.

For the first time, the reputation made structural sense.

It was not just stories about money and violence and people knowing his name.

Craig's scale was partly invisible.

Connor had been inside it without knowing.

"Did you know who I was when I got here?"

Craig laughed.

"Connor, I don't keep a scrapbook of every kid selling weed in Sussex."

Connor smiled despite himself.

"Fuck off."

"You asked."

"So when did you realise?"

Craig looked at him.

"Just now."

Connor studied his face.

"You're lying."

Craig ate the last bite of biscuit.

"Maybe prison's teaching you something after all."

## MALIK

Malik knew something had happened because Connor came looking for him.

Usually Malik went looking for information.

Information rarely arrived angry.

Connor did.

"You knew?"

Malik looked up.

"Morning to you too."

"About Mikey."

Malik's brain moved quickly.

"Which Mikey?"

Connor stared at him.

Malik sighed.

"All right. That one."

"Did you know?"

"Know what?"

"That he was connected to Craig."

Malik leaned back.

Interesting.

Very interesting.

"I suspected Craig recognised the name yesterday."

"You didn't even hear me say it."

"I don't need to hear everything."

"That's literally impossible."

"Yet here we are."

Connor shook his head.

"How many people knew?"

"Probably fewer than will claim they knew by dinner."

Connor sat down.

He looked genuinely unsettled.

Not frightened.

Recalibrating.

Malik understood that feeling.

The moment when the map in your head became obviously too small.

"I thought I was doing my own thing," Connor said.

Malik laughed.

"Everybody at the bottom thinks they're self-employed."

Connor looked at him.

"That's depressing."

"Most accurate things are."

"Craig said there were layers."

"There are always layers."

Connor went quiet.

Malik watched him.

This was the danger now.

Connor had not merely stood beside Craig during trouble.

He had discovered that Craig's world had touched his life before they ever met.

Prison loved meaning.

It would turn coincidence into destiny by lunchtime.

Malik decided not to help it.

For once.

## LEAH

Leah saw Craig and Connor talking for nearly forty minutes.

Forty minutes was not suspicious.

Prisoners had time.

That was the main resource of the institution.

Still, she noticed.

Connor came away looking different.

Less cocky than yesterday.

More thoughtful.

She stopped him later.

"Everything all right?"

Connor laughed.

"You lot only know one question."

"We have a handbook."

"Thought so."

He was more comfortable with her now too.

That should have been positive.

Leah found herself wondering whether Craig had encouraged it.

Then wondered why she was assigning Craig influence over every change in Connor.

The mythology was infecting her by stealth.

"You look like somebody told you something you didn't want to know," she said.

Connor considered that.

"Not didn't want to know. Just... didn't know I didn't know it."

Leah frowned.

"That's almost a sentence."

Connor smiled.

"You ever think you're doing your own thing, then find out you were part of someone else's thing the whole time?"

Leah's attention sharpened.

"What thing?"

Connor immediately realised he had said too much.

"Nothing."

Leah laughed.

"Now who's doing it?"

"Doing what?"

"Saying nothing and meaning a paragraph."

Connor shook his head.

"Been around these lot too long."

He walked off.

Craig was further down the landing.

Leah looked at him.

He looked back.

For a brief second neither looked away.

Then Craig gave the smallest nod.

Professional acknowledgement.

Nothing more.

Leah returned it.

And thought about it for longer than she should have.

## BENNETT

Bennett's problem had learned Craig's name.

That made everything worse.

The new message was still indirect.

**Your lot saying the kid's close to him now?**

Bennett did not need to ask which kid.

Connor.

He deleted the message.

Then stood in the toilet staring at himself in the mirror.

He had entered prison work believing the line between officers and prisoners was obvious.

Uniform.

Keys.

Doors.

Rules.

Now people outside were using him as a window into the building.

He had not agreed to that.

He had agreed to one favour.

Then another thing that had barely felt like a favour because he told himself it was harmless.

Now questions arrived.

Questions became expectations.

Expectations became obligations if you answered often enough.

Bennett splashed water on his face.

When he returned, Patel was waiting.

"Tom."

Bennett's stomach dropped.

"Yeah?"

"Can we have a chat later?"

"About?"

Patel looked at him.

"Later."

Then walked away.

Bennett stood still.

Nothing had happened.

Two words.

Have a chat.

His mind built a dismissal, an investigation and a prison sentence out of them before lunch.

For the first time, Bennett understood Connor's imaginary fifteen years.

Fear hated empty space.

It filled it itself.

## MASON

Mason listened to Connor explain the connection twice.

The second version was less coherent than the first.

"So basically I was selling Craig's weed."

"Not exactly."

"That's what I said."

"You said several layers."

"Yeah."

"Then it's not exactly."

Connor frowned.

"Whose side are you on?"

"Grammar's."

Connor laughed.

Mason liked the fact he could still laugh.

"It's mad though, innit?"

"Not really."

"How is that not mad?"

Mason shrugged.

"Young lads think the bloke handing them something is the whole business. Bigger businesses work because most people only see the bit they're standing in."

Connor looked at him.

"You knew?"

"No."

"Would you tell me if you did?"

"Probably not."

"Everyone in here is a prick."

"Now you're learning."

Connor smiled, then went serious.

"Makes sense why Craig's like... Craig though."

Mason watched him carefully.

"Careful."

"What now?"

"You just learned the bloke's bigger than you thought. Don't make him bigger than he is."

Connor frowned.

"What does that mean?"

"Means he's still a man."

"I know that."

"Do you?"

Connor looked towards Craig.

Mason followed his eyes.

Craig was talking to another prisoner, completely ordinary in posture, extraordinary only because of what everybody else attached to him.

"Respect people," Mason said. "Don't worship them. Prison's got enough religions."

Connor rolled his eyes.

"There he is. Old man."

Mason smiled.

"And there you are. Twenty-one."

## BIG SHARKY

Connor's name reached Big Sharky for the second time.

This time it came with context.

Young.

First timer.

Had stood beside Craig.

Apparently had some old connection into the edges of Craig's network without knowing it.

Big Sharky listened.

"Craig knows him?"

"Knows of the people around him. Something like that."

Sharky smiled.

Of course.

Craig had always been good at finding people who wanted to belong to something larger than themselves.

Big Sharky had once been good at it too.

Maybe that was where Craig learned.

Or maybe Sharky only liked remembering it that way.

"Anything else?" he asked.

"Craig's keeping him close."

Big Sharky looked at the wall.

Keeping him close.

The phrase annoyed him.

Not because of Connor.

Because it sounded familiar.

A younger Craig had once been kept close by older men.

Including Sharky.

That was the trouble with history.

It repeated badly and expected you not to notice.

"Leave the kid," Sharky said.

The caller paused.

"Yeah?"

"This isn't about him."

For once, Big Sharky was certain of that.

He ended the call.

Connor was not the problem.

Craig was.

Craig had always been the problem.

Or at least that was the story Big Sharky had spent years perfecting.
