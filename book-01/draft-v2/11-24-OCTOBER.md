# 24 OCTOBER

## CONNOR

Connor had spent most of his life believing money solved embarrassment.

No decent trainers? Money.

Mum short on a bill? Money.

Little brother wanted something everyone else at school had? Money.

People looked down on you? Get more money than them.

It was not a philosophy Connor had ever written down.

It was just the shape his life had taken.

Craig asked him about it while they were eating.

"Why'd you start?"

Connor looked up.

"Start what?"

"The line."

Connor shrugged.

"Money."

"Everyone says money."

"Because that's what it is."

Craig waited.

Connor sighed.

"Mum was always skint."

Craig said nothing.

"Not like starving or nothing," Connor added quickly. "Just... everything was hard. Everything was a conversation. New shoes was a conversation. School trip was a conversation. Shopping was a conversation. Gas was a conversation."

Craig nodded once.

Connor looked down at his food.

"Got sick of hearing no."

"So you decided nobody would say it to you."

Connor looked at him.

That was annoyingly accurate.

"Maybe."

"Brothers?"

"Yeah. Sister too. Little brother's the youngest."

"You buy them stuff?"

Connor smiled.

"Too much. Mum used to go mad."

"Why?"

"Said I was teaching them that things just appear."

Craig looked at him.

"She was right."

Connor frowned.

"Whose side are you on?"

"Your mum's, apparently."

Connor laughed.

Then Craig said, "You know what the problem is?"

"Go on. Everyone else gives me advice. May as well complete the set."

"You start making money for people you love. Then after a while you need them to need the money."

Connor stopped smiling.

Craig looked down at his tray.

"Because if they don't need it, you have to ask what all the other stuff was for."

For a few seconds Connor did not know what to say.

Craig had not spoken like somebody giving advice.

He had spoken like somebody remembering something.

"You talking about me or you?" Connor asked.

Craig smiled faintly.

"See? Prison's making you sharper."

"That's not an answer."

"You're obsessed with answers."

"Because no one gives me any."

Craig leaned back.

"You remind me of someone."

Connor stared.

"Who?"

"Me."

Connor laughed.

Craig didn't.

The laugh died.

"Seriously?"

"Bit younger. Bit louder. Thought knowing five people meant I knew the world."

Connor smiled despite himself.

"I know more than five people."

"Exactly what he'd have said."

Connor shook his head.

"So what happened to him?"

Craig looked around the prison.

"You're looking at him."

That answer stayed with Connor all afternoon.

## MACCA

Macca got a drawing in the post.

His daughter had drawn the family.

Mum.

Her.

Her little brother.

The dog.

Macca stood at the far end wearing what appeared to be a crown.

He smiled.

Then read the writing underneath.

**DAD IN HIS CASTLE.**

His partner had apparently told the kids he was staying somewhere old.

Not exactly a lie.

Darren looked over.

"What's that?"

Macca showed him.

Darren smiled.

"Castle's decent."

"I'm a king."

"Craig'll be fuming."

Macca laughed.

It was the first Craig joke he had made.

That annoyed him slightly.

The man's name had entered everything.

Still, the drawing mattered more.

Macca folded it carefully and put it somewhere safe.

"You ever think about getting out and your kids being different?" he asked.

Darren did not answer immediately.

"Yeah."

"Like you've got the version in your head, but they're carrying on becoming somebody."

Darren nodded.

Macca thought of Connor, who had spent half the week talking about a little brother.

Prison did not freeze anybody.

It only froze your access to them.

## LEAH

Leah saw Craig and Connor talking through most of lunch.

There was nothing unusual about that now.

That was what was unusual.

In less than two weeks, Connor had gone from frightened new arrival to somebody whose place beside Craig looked natural.

Leah had seen prison friendships form quickly before.

Sometimes necessity accelerated intimacy.

A man could tell a cellmate something on night three that he had never told his closest friend outside because the cellmate was physically there when the mask fell off.

Still, this felt different.

Connor listened to Craig.

Actually listened.

That in itself was remarkable because Connor usually looked like he was preparing his next sentence while somebody else spoke.

Craig said something.

Connor went quiet.

Leah watched the expression change.

"You're doing it again," Patel said.

Leah looked at him.

"Doing what?"

"Watching Craig."

"I'm watching Connor."

Patel smiled.

"Course."

Leah felt irritation arrive too quickly.

"He was involved in an incident four days ago."

"I know."

"So I'm monitoring him."

"I didn't accuse you of anything."

"Good."

Patel went back to his work.

Leah looked down at hers.

Then, against her better judgement, back at the wing.

Craig had gone.

Connor was still there, staring at his tray as if somebody had said something important.

Leah wanted to know what.

That was the first moment she admitted curiosity had moved beyond paperwork.

She did not like the admission.

## JAY

The outside pressure had become quieter.

Jay trusted quiet less than noise.

Noise meant somebody wanted a reaction immediately.

Quiet meant people were thinking.

Rico was keeping his head down.

Deano's lot had publicly distanced themselves from the whole thing without saying so publicly.

Leon continued behaving as though the gym could declare independence from the rest of the prison.

Malik knew something and refused to tell Jay because Malik enjoyed revenge in petty forms.

Normality was returning.

That was exactly when Jay expected the next problem.

He saw Connor alone and sat beside him.

Connor looked surprised.

"All right?"

"Yeah."

Jay smiled.

"You're the most all-right man in England."

Connor laughed.

"Everyone keeps asking."

"You know why?"

"Because none of you have conversation skills."

"That too."

Jay looked around.

"You know what happened the other day wasn't random, yeah?"

Connor's smile faded.

"I guessed."

"Good."

"You know why?"

Jay considered the question.

He knew pieces.

Not enough.

"Old problems."

"Craig's?"

"Yeah."

"With who?"

Jay smiled.

"There you are. Asking too much."

Connor sighed dramatically.

"This place is ridiculous."

"True."

Jay stood.

"Just remember: because you stood with him once doesn't mean you owe everybody around him forever."

Connor looked up.

"Why are you telling me that?"

Jay thought of Rico.

"Because people confuse loyalty with letting somebody else choose your consequences."

He walked away before Connor could ask more.

## BENNETT

Patel's chat turned out to be about attendance.

Bennett nearly laughed from relief.

"You've had a few late starts," Patel said.

"Dad."

"I know. I'm not having a go. I'm asking if you need support."

Bennett nodded.

"I'm fine."

Patel leaned back.

"Tom."

There was enough kindness in his voice to make Bennett feel worse.

"If there's something else going on, you can tell me before it becomes a work problem."

Bennett looked at him.

For one dangerous second, the truth got very close to his mouth.

Money.

Pressure.

Messages.

One favour becoming several.

People asking about Craig.

His father's address sitting in other people's phones.

Bennett imagined the relief of putting it all on the desk.

Then imagined the investigation.

Career gone.

Police.

His father finding out.

Prisoners finding out.

The words retreated.

"Just Dad," Bennett said.

Patel watched him.

"All right."

Bennett knew he did not believe him.

He left the office feeling as though he had just walked past an exit.

## CONNOR

Connor rang home that evening.

His little brother answered before his mum could stop him.

"Castle boy!"

Connor laughed.

"Who told you that?"

"Mum said it's old."

"It's not a castle."

"Got towers?"

Connor thought of the old walls.

"Sort of."

"Castle."

Connor let him have it.

His mum came on.

They talked about court, though there was nothing new.

They talked about money, which made Connor feel useless.

They talked about his girlfriend, who had apparently been round once and promised she would visit when she could.

Connor held onto that.

Then his mum said, "Your brother keeps asking when you're back."

Connor closed his eyes.

"What do you tell him?"

"I don't know, Connor. What am I supposed to tell him?"

He heard the tiredness again.

"Tell him soon."

"Is it?"

Connor stared at the wall.

Five years did not exist yet.

Only fear did.

Ten.

Twelve.

Fifteen.

"I don't know."

His mum sighed.

"That's the first sensible thing you've said."

Connor smiled sadly.

After the call he found Craig sitting alone.

"My mum reckons I've never said anything sensible."

Craig looked up.

"I like her already."

Connor sat down.

"You know what you said earlier?"

"I've said loads."

"About me reminding you of you."

Craig's expression became unreadable again.

"What about it?"

"Did you go prison young?"

"Young enough."

"Did it change you?"

Craig looked around.

"Everything changes you. Question is whether you notice while it's happening."

Connor frowned.

"That's another Mason answer."

"I'm getting old."

"How old are you?"

Craig smiled.

"Nice try."

Connor laughed.

Then sat quietly beside him.

For once, he did not ask another question.

The opening week of his prison life was over.

He still did not know what sentence he would receive.

He did not know why Big Sharky wanted Craig hurt.

He did not know his girlfriend would eventually stop waiting.

He did not know how much he would come to enjoy the reputation that had attached itself to him—or what it would cost.

He knew only this:

A week ago he had entered Lewes terrified of everybody.

Now people knew his name.

That felt like progress.

It wasn't.

Not entirely.
