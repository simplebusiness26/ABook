# 15 OCTOBER

## MASON

Mason could spot a first-timer before the first-timer had worked out where the toilets were.

There were signs.

Too much looking without wanting to be seen looking. Too much listening. Every buzzer treated like an announcement from God. Every officer's instruction received as though there might be a hidden second meaning.

Connor had all of them.

He had spent reception trying not to stare at Mason and the other orderly, which meant he had stared at them almost constantly.

Mason found this flattering.

Connor probably had him down as a lifer. Maybe double figures. Maybe some terrifying old-school criminal who had been sitting in Lewes since televisions had backs on them.

The truth was less impressive.

Mason had simply been to prison too many times.

There was a difference.

Outside, Mason forgot appointments, ignored letters and could somehow turn a perfectly manageable Tuesday into a disaster by lunchtime.

Inside, he knew where he was meant to be.

He knew which officer's voice meant *now* and which meant *when I can be bothered to ask again*.

He knew the sound of a door opening for him and a door opening for somebody else.

He knew that the first night always made young men look younger.

Connor looked about seventeen this morning.

He was standing in a queue holding a plastic cup as though somebody might test him on it.

"You survived, then," Mason said.

Connor turned.

For half a second he looked relieved to see him.

Then he remembered he was twenty-one and apparently not allowed to be relieved by anything.

"Course."

"Good sleep?"

Connor stared at him.

Mason nodded.

"Stupid question."

"Some bloke was shouting for about three hours."

"Only three? Quiet night."

Connor wasn't sure if he was joking.

Mason let him wonder.

Across the room, another prisoner said Craig's name.

Not loudly.

That was the thing.

Nobody needed to say it loudly.

The name had done most of the work already.

Mason glanced over.

Craig stood near an officer waiting for something to be sorted. He looked exactly as he had the night before: no performance, no visible interest in the attention around him, no attempt to prove that the stories were true.

Mason had always thought reputation was strangest when the person carrying it seemed least impressed by it.

Connor followed his eyes.

"Everyone still staring at him?"

"Including you."

Connor looked away.

"I'm not staring."

"You're studying him academically."

"I just don't get it."

"What?"

"He's just a bloke."

Mason smiled.

"Most people are."

Connor frowned as though Mason had deliberately failed to answer.

He had.

Mason had been inside long enough to know when information helped and when it merely gave a young man a bigger problem to think about.

"What's he done?" Connor asked.

"Depends who you ask."

"What are people saying?"

"Conspiracy."

"What conspiracy?"

Mason shrugged.

"The big one."

Connor stared at him.

Mason laughed.

"That's genuinely about the level of information in here."

One man had Craig controlling half the south coast by breakfast. Another said he had been finished for years. A third was certain he knew Craig personally because his cousin once stood beside somebody at a boxing event who knew somebody from London.

Prison did not need the internet.

It had men with nothing to do.

"Listen," Mason said. "One bit of advice."

Connor looked suspicious immediately.

"What?"

"Don't collect other people's problems."

"You said that yesterday."

"Must be important then."

"I ain't collecting nothing."

Mason looked over at Craig, then back at Connor.

"Good."

Connor followed the look again.

Mason thought: *This is going well.*

## LEAH

Officer Leah Mercer disliked famous prisoners before she met them.

Not because of the prisoners.

Because of everybody else.

A name arrived and suddenly ordinary adults began behaving like schoolchildren who had heard a celebrity was visiting.

Prisoners asked questions they already knew nobody would answer. Officers who had been working perfectly normally five minutes earlier became oddly interested in paperwork happening at the other end of a room. Everybody claimed not to care.

Everybody cared enough to mention that they did not care.

Craig was today's version.

Leah read what she needed to read and ignored the rest.

Allegations. Risk information. A serious organised-crime case. A prosecution position that placed him high in a large drug-supply conspiracy. Money matters attached around the edges. Connections. Violence in the intelligence picture. Too many names linked to too many other names.

Nothing in the paperwork required the word *legend*.

That was helpful.

She watched him answer an officer's question without argument.

No swagger.

No unnecessary joke.

No attempt to establish who he was.

That made him easier and harder at the same time.

The men who announced themselves were tiring but obvious.

The quiet ones made you work.

"He's got half the wing talking," Bennett said beside her.

Leah looked at him.

"And now you."

"I'm observing."

"So are they. Apparently."

Bennett smiled but it disappeared quickly.

Leah noticed that.

Not because of Craig.

Bennett had been slightly off for days. Checking his phone too often before coming through the gate. Distracted in conversations. Once, yesterday, she had asked him the same question twice and watched him answer the second version as if hearing it for the first time.

People brought outside life into prison even when they thought they had left it in the staff car park.

Prisoners did too.

That was one of the great lies of the place: the walls divided inside from outside.

They didn't.

They only made the journey between them more complicated.

Across the room Connor stood with his shoulders slightly too high.

Leah recognised him from reception.

Young. First time. Trying desperately not to look first time.

She watched him glance at Craig and then at Mason.

"That lad's going to exhaust himself," she said.

Bennett followed her gaze.

"Which one?"

"Connor."

"What about him?"

"He's trying to notice everything."

"First night."

"Exactly."

Connor caught her looking and immediately looked away.

Leah almost smiled.

Then Craig said something to the officer beside him, and two prisoners across the room went quiet at exactly the same time.

Leah did not hear what he said.

She noticed the silence.

That was more useful.

## CONNOR

The first phone call mattered so much that Connor nearly ruined it by thinking too hard about what to say.

He had rehearsed it in his head.

*I'm all right.*

Obviously he wasn't all right.

*Don't worry.*

Obviously they were going to worry.

*It's not as bad as it sounds.*

He had no idea if that was true.

By the time he actually heard his mum's voice, the speech disappeared.

"Mum?"

There was a pause.

Then: "Connor."

That was it.

One word.

It nearly finished him.

He looked at the wall because there was nobody there he wanted to see his face.

"You all right?"

It was the stupidest question he had ever asked her.

She laughed once, but it wasn't a laugh.

"Am *I* all right?"

"You know what I mean."

"Where are you?"

"Lewes."

Another pause.

He could hear movement in the background. A television. Somebody asking something. His little brother, maybe.

Connor closed his eyes.

"Is he there?"

His mum knew exactly who he meant.

"He's here."

"Don't put him on yet."

"Why?"

Connor didn't know.

Because hearing him might make this real in a way gates and court and handcuffs somehow had not.

Because he had bought that kid trainers two weeks ago and had enjoyed the way he had strutted around the kitchen in them like he had personally signed for Chelsea.

Because fifteen years had done its maths in Connor's head again during the night.

"Just— in a minute."

His mum went quiet.

Then softer: "What happened?"

Connor stared at his healed middle finger.

"They got blood."

"Blood?"

"From the car."

"Connor..."

He hated the way she said his name.

Not angry.

Worse.

Tired.

Like all the money he had brought home had suddenly become very expensive.

"Solicitor said we'll see."

"And the gun?"

Connor stiffened.

"Who told you that?"

"It's what they're saying."

Of course it was.

Outside had gossip too.

"They ain't found one."

"Was there one?"

Connor looked towards the door.

"Mum."

"I'm asking you."

"Not on the phone."

Silence.

He wished immediately that he had said something else.

"Put him on," Connor said.

A few seconds later his little brother came on sounding excited because Connor was on a phone, not because Connor was in prison.

"Where are you?"

Connor smiled despite himself.

"At a hotel."

His mum said his name sharply in the background.

His brother laughed.

"Mum said prison."

"Yeah, well, terrible reviews."

"You got a PlayStation?"

"No."

"That's dead."

Connor laughed.

Actually laughed.

For twenty seconds he was just a brother again.

Then somebody indicated that time was moving.

Connor's throat tightened.

"Listen, be good for Mum, yeah?"

"I am."

"No, actually be good. Not your version."

His brother giggled.

"When you coming home?"

There it was.

Connor looked at the wall.

Ten.

Twelve.

Fifteen.

"Soon as I can."

After the call ended he stood with the receiver still near his face for a second too long.

"They charge by the minute after you've hung up, do they?"

Connor turned.

Craig was waiting nearby.

Connor put the phone down.

"Funny."

"Wasn't bad."

Up close, Craig looked even less like Connor expected.

There should have been something visible, Connor thought. A mark. A sign. Something to explain why grown men kept lowering their voices around him.

Instead there was just a man waiting to use a phone.

"Your kid?" Craig asked.

"Brother."

"Sounded young."

"He is."

Craig nodded once.

"Worst bit."

Connor looked at him.

"What?"

"Them growing while you're in here."

For the first time since hearing Craig's name, Connor forgot to wonder who he was.

"You got kids?"

Craig gave him a look.

Not angry.

Just enough to remind Connor that a question could be ordinary and still be none of his business.

"Sorry."

"You're new," Craig said.

"That obvious?"

Craig glanced at Connor's posture.

"Only from space."

Connor laughed before he could stop himself.

Craig picked up the receiver.

"Relax your shoulders, Connor. You're going to pull something."

Connor walked away annoyed that he immediately relaxed his shoulders.

## MALIK

Malik liked information best when it belonged to somebody else.

That was safer.

The perfect piece of prison information was interesting enough to trade, harmless enough not to get punched over, and vague enough that if it turned out to be nonsense you could blame the person who told you.

Craig was not perfect information.

Craig was dangerous information.

By lunchtime, Malik had heard five versions of why he was at Lewes.

By afternoon, he had heard seven.

The eighth arrived from outside.

Not directly.

Nothing useful ever arrived directly.

A friend called somebody. Somebody mentioned that a man in Brighton had been asking who had landed at Lewes. Another name came up. Then another. The question underneath all of it was simple.

*Is Craig really there?*

Malik sat on the edge of his bunk while his cellmate watched television.

"You listening?" the cellmate asked.

"Yeah."

"What did he just say?"

Malik looked at the screen.

A man in a suit was pointing at a house.

"House is damp."

"He said roof."

"Same family of problems."

His cellmate shook his head.

Malik wasn't watching.

He was thinking about the question.

Is Craig really there?

People asked questions for reasons.

Sometimes they wanted gossip.

Sometimes they wanted access.

Sometimes they wanted somebody found.

Malik had spent enough time around people who communicated indirectly to know when a question was carrying another question behind it.

He did not yet know what the second question was.

That bothered him.

"What's up with you?" his cellmate said.

"Nothing."

"You got that face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're pretending you don't know something."

Malik smiled.

"That's just my face."

A shout came from somewhere down the landing.

Somebody answered.

Then somebody else said Craig's name.

Malik looked towards the door.

One day in.

Already outside was asking inside about him.

That did not mean anything by itself.

But Malik had survived long enough by respecting the words *by itself*.

He leaned back against the wall.

"You heard anything about Craig?" his cellmate asked.

Malik looked at the television.

"Only rubbish."

"Like what?"

"Exactly."

He did not say that his phone call had felt different.

He did not say that somebody outside cared enough to confirm Craig's location.

Information was only useful if you decided when it became public.

For now, Malik kept it.

Across Lewes, men were telling stories about Craig.

Malik was beginning to wonder who was listening.
