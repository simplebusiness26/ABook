# TUESDAY 3 DECEMBER 2019 — TINSEL

## CONNOR — C WING

The first Christmas decoration on C Wing was a strip of silver tinsel cable-tied to a noticeboard.

Connor stared at it.

"That's bleak."

Bruce appeared beside him.

"Festive austerity."

"It looks like someone decorated a bus stop."

"Exactly. Public-sector Christmas."

Connor laughed.

Within two days there were paper chains, a cardboard Santa with one bent leg and a small tree that looked as if it had survived several previous Decembers through spite alone.

The strange thing was how much people cared.

Men who claimed Christmas was for kids argued over where the tree should go.

Men who said they hated decorations complained that one landing had more than another.

Someone started a rumour that Christmas dinner would be terrible.

Someone else said it was decent last year.

Nobody could remember who had actually been there last year.

Prison made expertise easy. You only needed confidence and one person willing to listen.

Connor pushed his cleaning trolley beneath the paper chains.

"Rush," somebody called.

He looked up automatically.

Then saw Stuart watching him from the cell doorway.

"What?"

Stuart smiled.

"Nothing."

"You've got a face."

"Apparently everybody here is a face expert."

Connor pointed at him.

"Don't steal my lines."

Stuart laughed.

"You answer to it very quickly."

"People shout it all day."

"Yes."

"So?"

Stuart shrugged.

"Nothing."

Connor hated when Stuart used nothing properly.

It gave him nowhere to argue.

---

## MACCA

The school play was Friday.

Macca had known the date for three weeks.

Friday 6 December.

Six o'clock.

His daughter was a star.

Not the star.

A star.

Macca had corrected three people about that because accuracy mattered when you had missed enough things already.

"She's got one line," he told Connor.

"What's the line?"

Macca looked offended.

"You think I don't know?"

"I was asking."

Macca lowered his voice theatrically.

"Follow the light."

Connor nodded.

"Big line."

"Massive. Whole production falls apart without it."

"Obviously."

Macca smiled.

Then the smile went.

His partner had promised to ring afterwards.

Not during. Afterwards.

Tell him how it went.

Let his daughter tell him herself if she was not too tired.

Macca had asked three times what time they would be home.

He hated himself for asking three times.

Outside, he would have just been there.

Inside, every family event became logistics.

Phone slots.

Association.

Lock-up.

Staffing.

Whether somebody else's problem closed the wing at the wrong hour.

Connor saw the change in his face.

"You'll get the call."

Macca looked at him.

"You don't know that."

Connor nodded.

"No."

That was better than pretending.

Macca appreciated it.

---

## JOSH AND DARREN

Josh had decided the tree needed lights.

Darren had decided Josh needed a hobby that did not involve improving prison property.

"It's depressing," Josh said.

"It's a tree."

"It's got no lights."

"You're in prison, not Lapland."

Josh pointed at the cardboard Santa.

"Then explain him."

Darren looked at Santa.

"Hostage situation."

Josh laughed loudly.

For all his excitement, Christmas had made him talk about home more.

His mum did a big dinner every year.

His younger sister always opened something early and blamed somebody else.

His dad sometimes appeared, sometimes did not.

Josh told those stories like weather reports rather than wounds.

Darren understood enough not to ask which years the dad had come.

"Mum'll save me food," Josh said.

"Till when?"

"Till I get out."

Darren laughed.

"That's gonna be a dangerous turkey."

"Freezer, mate. Technology."

"Course."

Josh grinned.

Darren looked at him and felt the same uncomfortable pride he had been feeling for weeks.

Josh knew the wing now.

Knew routines.

Knew when Macca wanted quiet for a phone call.

Knew not to ask Jamo about visits orderly unless Jamo mentioned it first.

Knew Stuart would answer any question seriously if you asked it with a straight enough face.

Knew Bruce should never be asked a question if you needed the answer quickly.

A lot of that came from Darren.

It felt good being useful to somebody.

Craig had taught Connor the difference between useful and important.

Darren had never had that lesson.

---

## OFFICER MERCER

Christmas made the prison softer and sharper at the same time.

Mercer had seen it before.

More cards.

More calls.

More men trying to pretend a cancelled visit did not matter.

More arguments over ridiculous things because the real thing people were angry about lived twenty miles away.

She watched Macca check the phone area twice in ten minutes and knew exactly what date was in his head.

She watched Josh discussing decorations as if the government had appointed him creative director.

She watched Connor laugh with Jamo despite the cell move that had seemed permanent only weeks earlier.

And she watched Craig.

That was the part she had started monitoring in herself.

Not him.

Herself.

He was reading near the rail.

Nothing remarkable.

Mercer knew his court dates were increasing after Christmas.

She knew his case was moving.

That was professional information.

She also knew he preferred tea without much milk because she had noticed it three times.

That was not information she needed.

She disliked the distinction.

Craig looked up.

Their eyes met.

"Officer Mercer."

"Craig."

"Tree's leaning."

Mercer looked at it.

It was.

"Security threat?"

"Potentially."

"I'll alert the governor."

He almost smiled.

Mercer walked on before the conversation became anything more than ordinary.

She had begun thinking about ordinary conversations afterwards.

That was the problem.

---

## CONNOR — NIGHT

Stuart was reading when Connor climbed onto the top bunk.

"Do you like Christmas?" Connor asked.

"Yes."

"Because Jesus?"

Stuart looked up.

"That is traditionally involved."

Connor laughed.

"I mean, proper religious Christmas."

"Family too. Food. Church. Home."

The last word changed his voice slightly.

Connor heard it.

"You speak to your family much?"

"When I can."

"They abroad?"

Stuart nodded.

Connor had known Stuart was African-born. He had not spent much time thinking about what that meant at Christmas.

Different country.

Different time zone.

A family even farther from Lewes than Connor's.

"You miss them?"

Stuart closed the book.

"Very much."

Connor lay back.

Same cell.

Different distance.

The paper chains outside rustled when somebody passed.

Prison was trying to look festive.

It had not yet worked.


# FRIDAY 6 DECEMBER 2019 — LOUD ENOUGH

## MACCA — C WING

At six o'clock Macca's daughter was standing on a school stage dressed as a silver star.

At six o'clock Macca was behind a prison door.

He knew because he had checked the time so often that the numbers had stopped being useful.

Five fifty-three.

Five fifty-seven.

Six.

Somewhere outside, parents were finding seats, teachers were shushing children and somebody was probably realising a costume had broken.

Macca pictured all of it from memory.

The school hall.

Plastic chairs.

Bad coffee.

Children waving at parents when they were meant to be acting.

His daughter searching the room even though she knew he would not be there.

That last thought was the one he tried not to picture.

His cellmate had the television low without being asked.

Macca appreciated that enough not to say it.

At six fifteen he whispered her line to himself.

"Follow the light."

Then felt like an idiot.

Then wished he could hear it.

---

## CONNOR

Connor knew better than to joke with Macca that evening.

That was progress.

Two months earlier he would have tried to make somebody laugh because silence made him uncomfortable.

Now he could recognise when humour belonged somewhere else.

He sat with Jamo during association, both of them occasionally glancing at Macca.

"Six o'clock, weren't it?" Jamo said.

Connor nodded.

"Yeah."

"My little sister did one once. I got dragged there. Thought it was gonna be shit."

"Was it?"

"Obviously. Best shit thing ever though."

Connor smiled.

Macca came out when the doors opened later.

He went straight to the phone.

His partner answered.

Connor moved away without needing to be told.

---

## MACCA — PHONE

"How was it?"

Macca did not say hello.

His partner laughed softly.

"Hello to you too."

"How was it?"

"She was brilliant."

Macca closed his eyes.

"Did she say it?"

"Of course she said it."

"Loud?"

There was a pause.

His partner's voice changed.

"Very loud."

Macca swallowed.

"Put her on."

"She's here."

Then the little voice.

"Dad?"

Macca gripped the receiver tighter.

"Star."

She giggled.

"I did it loud."

"I know."

"Did you hear?"

The question was completely serious.

Macca looked at the prison wall.

There were lies you told children because you were weak.

There were lies you told because truth had no useful job in the moment.

"Yeah," he said. "Course I did."

She sounded delighted.

"I knew you would."

Macca covered his eyes with his free hand.

Behind him the wing carried on.

Someone arguing.

Someone laughing.

A door shutting.

He listened to his daughter describe the play in the wrong order.

A boy forgot a line.

One angel fell over.

There was a song she did not like.

Someone's hat came off.

Her line had gone perfectly.

Macca listened to every detail.

He could not get the evening back.

But he could refuse to miss the story as well.

---

## OFFICER MERCER

Mercer saw Macca crying after the call.

Not dramatically.

He turned away, wiped his face once and stood still until he trusted it again.

She pretended not to see.

Professional kindness was sometimes an action.

Sometimes it was leaving somebody a private thirty seconds in a public place.

Craig was nearby.

He had seen too.

He did the same thing.

Looked away.

Mercer noticed that.

Of course she did.

She was beginning to notice things about Craig that had nothing to do with his risk file.

She told herself the observation was still relevant.

Men with reputations were often easiest to understand by what they did when nobody needed to know they had done anything.

That sounded professional enough to keep.

---

## BRUCE

Bruce found Macca later.

"Heard your kid smashed it."

Macca looked at him.

"How the fuck do you know?"

Bruce shrugged.

"Information network."

"Connor told you."

"Part of the network."

Macca smiled despite himself.

Bruce reached into his pocket and produced a badly folded paper star.

"Made this."

Macca stared at it.

The star had seven points, none the same length.

"That's terrible."

"Handmade. Adds value."

Macca took it.

"Why?"

Bruce looked uncomfortable for perhaps the first time all month.

"Because she was a star. Keep up."

Then he walked away before Macca could thank him.

Macca looked at the paper shape.

Bruce had written on the back:

**LOUD ENOUGH.**

Macca put it beside his daughter's drawing.


# MONDAY 9 DECEMBER 2019 — VISITING DAY

## CONNOR — VISITS

Connor's second proper family visit hurt less before it happened and more while it was happening.

He knew the room now.

Knew the process.

Knew what the chairs felt like.

Knew how quickly the time went once the people you loved were actually sitting opposite you.

The first visit had been shock.

This one was comparison.

His mum looked tired.

His girlfriend looked good.

That annoyed him for half a second and then annoyed him more for being annoyed.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing."

His mum looked between them.

"Don't start."

Connor laughed.

"I didn't say anything."

"I raised you. I can hear the things you haven't said yet."

His girlfriend smiled.

For a while they talked normally.

His little brother's Christmas list.

His sister arguing with somebody at school.

One of his brothers doing something stupid with a bike.

A bill his mum insisted was sorted even though Connor could hear from her voice that it probably was not.

Outside life arrived in pieces small enough to fit across a prison table.

Connor wanted all of it.

Even the boring bits.

Especially the boring bits.

His girlfriend was quieter than last time.

Not cold.

Just slightly elsewhere.

Connor noticed every pause.

He did not interrogate them.

That took more effort than he wanted anybody to know.

"You going out Christmas?" he asked.

"Maybe."

"Where?"

She named somewhere with friends.

Connor knew some of the names.

Not all.

A familiar old feeling rose.

Who are they?

What blokes are going?

Why didn't you say before?

Stuart's sentence returned: fear loved empty spaces.

Connor drank from the paper cup.

"Have a good time."

His girlfriend looked surprised.

"You mean that?"

Connor smiled.

"Mostly."

She laughed.

The honesty worked better than pretending he did not care.

---

## CONNOR AND MUM

Near the end his girlfriend went to get something from the vending area with Connor's mum.

His mum came back first.

She sat down.

"You all right with her?"

Connor looked over.

"Why?"

"Just asking."

"Mum."

She gave him the same look Mercer gave prisoners when they answered too quickly.

"People's lives keep moving," she said.

Connor felt his chest tighten.

"What's that meant to mean?"

"Exactly what I said."

"Has she said something?"

"No."

"Then why say it?"

His mum leaned forward.

"Because you can't control home from in here. Don't drive yourself mad trying."

Connor stared at her.

He wanted reassurance.

She's fine.

She'll wait.

Nothing will change.

His mum was not giving him that because she loved him more than she loved making him comfortable.

"All right," Connor said.

"And stop trying to buy everyone Christmas from prison."

Connor laughed despite himself.

"I knew that was coming."

"Your brother wants you, not trainers."

That landed again.

Prison repeated lessons until you stopped finding new ways not to hear them.

---

## JOSH — VISITS HALL

Josh had a visit the same day.

Connor saw him across the room with his mum and a younger girl Connor assumed was his sister.

Josh looked different around them.

Still talking too fast.

Still moving his hands.

But softer.

His sister had brought a drawing of a car.

Of course she had.

Josh held it up to the light as if assessing engineering drawings.

Darren had no visit that day.

Back on the wing, Josh returned carrying nothing physical except the energy of having seen home.

"My sister drew an M3," he announced.

Darren looked at the picture.

"That's a rectangle with circles."

"She's eight."

"Then it's excellent."

Josh smiled.

"Mum says I look skinny."

"Mums think prison steals weight through the walls."

"She tried to feed me the whole visit."

"Good woman."

Josh sat beside him.

Then went quiet for perhaps twenty seconds.

Darren noticed because Josh's silences were events.

"What?"

Josh looked at the floor.

"Just weird them leaving."

Darren nodded.

"Yeah."

There was no joke for that one.

So Darren stayed beside him until Josh found one himself.

---

## STUART

Connor came back from visits full of thoughts he did not want.

Stuart looked up from the Bible.

"Good visit?"

"Yeah."

"That sounded like a complicated yes."

Connor lay on the bunk.

"Girlfriend's going out over Christmas."

Stuart waited.

"You're meant to say something."

"You have not told me the problem yet."

Connor frowned.

"She's going out."

"Yes."

"While I'm in here."

"Also yes."

Connor stared at him.

"You're terrible at this."

Stuart smiled.

"Would you prefer I tell you she must remain indoors because you are in prison?"

Connor laughed despite himself.

"No."

"Then perhaps the problem is you are frightened life will continue without asking permission."

Connor stopped laughing.

"You ever get tired of being right in a boring way?"

"Never."

Connor turned onto his back.

Life outside had not agreed to pause.

He had known that for weeks.

Knowing and accepting were different jobs.


# FRIDAY 13 DECEMBER 2019 — CHRISTMAS CANTEEN

## JOSH

Christmas canteen became Josh's World Cup.

He had a list.

Darren discovered this when Josh unfolded a piece of paper long enough to qualify as correspondence.

"What's that?"

"Plan."

"For what?"

"Christmas."

Darren took it.

Chocolate.

Crisps.

Biscuits.

Noodles.

Drinks.

More chocolate.

Something crossed out and rewritten twice.

"You planning to feed the landing?"

"Might."

"With what money?"

"Budgeting."

Darren laughed.

"You wrote budgeting at the top. That doesn't make it budgeting."

"It's financially organised."

Bruce leaned over Darren's shoulder.

"No emergency cheese allocation."

Josh looked at him.

"Why would I need emergency cheese?"

Bruce stared back.

"That's why it's an emergency."

Darren handed the list back.

"Ignore him."

Josh added **CHEESE?** at the bottom.

Darren closed his eyes.

## CONNOR — WING CLEANER

Connor's Christmas humiliation was glitter.

Somebody had introduced glitter into C Wing through a legitimate craft activity and the prison had immediately proved humanity was not responsible enough for it.

It was everywhere.

Floor.

Tables.

Connor's trousers.

His mop seemed to distribute it rather than remove it.

Bruce walked past, looked at Connor's sparkling shoes and stopped.

"Rush: festive edition."

"Say one more thing."

"You look magical."

Connor chased him three steps with the mop before remembering an officer was watching.

Officer Mercer raised an eyebrow.

Connor stopped.

"Workplace disagreement."

"Clean the floor, Blake."

"Trying."

Mercer looked at the glitter.

"Try harder."

Connor muttered something.

"What was that?"

"Merry Christmas, miss."

Mercer almost smiled.

"Thought so."

Prison reputation had not protected Connor from glitter.

He found that reassuring in a strange way.

---

## DARREN

Darren had a bad afternoon.

Not dramatic.

That was the problem.

The bad days that frightened him most were the ones nobody else would have recognised.

Restless body.

Thoughts narrowing.

Every ordinary irritation becoming a reason to make the day disappear faster.

Josh was beside him talking about canteen.

Normally Darren found it funny.

Today the words felt too loud.

"Mate," Darren said sharply. "Give it a rest."

Josh stopped.

The silence landed immediately.

Darren regretted the tone.

Josh looked away.

"All right."

Darren rubbed his face.

"Sorry."

"It's fine."

"No, I was being a prick."

Josh shrugged.

Darren recognised the shrug because he had used it himself for years.

A way of pretending something had not touched you.

"Come here."

Josh looked suspicious.

"What?"

Darren handed him a chocolate bar from his own canteen.

Josh stared at it.

"Bribery?"

"Apology."

"Same thing sometimes."

Darren laughed.

"Take it before I change my mind."

Josh did.

The moment repaired easily.

That should have comforted Darren.

Instead he found himself thinking about how much of Josh's prison now ran through him.

Mood.

People.

Habits.

The places he sat.

What he treated as normal.

Being looked up to had felt good when Darren only counted the good things he passed on.

---

## OFFICER BENNETT

Bennett received another message after shift.

This one contained no threat.

That made it worse.

It referred to Christmas.

Family time.

How unfortunate it would be if problems followed him into it.

Bennett sat in his car without starting the engine.

The phone was in his hand.

He thought about showing somebody.

Patel.

Mercer.

Anybody.

Then he pictured the questions.

How long has this been happening?

What have you already done?

Why didn't you report it earlier?

Every day Bennett waited made the first honest sentence more expensive.

A tap on the window made him jump violently.

Mercer stood outside.

Bennett lowered the glass.

"Jesus."

"Sorry."

She looked at him.

"You sitting here for a reason?"

"Just checking something."

Her eyes moved to the phone.

Bennett turned it face-down.

Too obvious.

Mercer noticed.

Of course she did.

"Bennett."

"I'm fine."

She stared at him.

"That's becoming insulting."

He laughed once.

It sounded bad.

Mercer rested a hand on the edge of the window.

"If something's wrong, waiting normally doesn't make it smaller."

Bennett looked ahead.

"I know."

For one second Mercer thought he might continue.

He did not.

"See you Monday," he said.

Mercer stepped back.

Bennett drove home with the message deleted and memorised.

---

## OFFICER MERCER

Mercer watched his car leave.

Craig's sentence from weeks earlier returned.

*He looks frightened.*

She hated that Craig had seen it before she had been willing to name it.

She hated more that part of her wanted to ask what else he saw.

That was not a reason to speak to a prisoner.

It was exactly the sort of reason she needed to distrust.

Mercer went back inside.

Christmas lights had been added around the staff noticeboard too.

They blinked cheerfully above a list of staffing problems.

Public-sector Christmas, she thought.

Then wondered where she had heard that phrase.

Probably Bruce.

That made it worse.


# SUNDAY 15 DECEMBER 2019 — CARDS

## CONNOR

Connor received three Christmas cards on the same morning.

One from his mum.

One from his little brother.

One signed by several people who had apparently discovered group stationery.

His girlfriend's name was on the third card, squeezed near the bottom.

Connor noticed immediately.

He hated himself for noticing immediately.

His little brother's card was the best.

A drawing of Connor standing beside a sports car in front of a house far larger than any house Connor had ever lived in.

Across the top:

**WHEN YOU COME HOME**

No date.

No sentence.

No legal process.

Just when.

Connor sat on his bunk looking at it.

Stuart saw his face.

"Good?"

Connor handed him the card.

Stuart smiled.

"He has confidence in your future car."

"Kid's got taste."

"And your arms."

Connor looked.

The drawn Connor had enormous square arms.

"Accurate."

Stuart laughed.

Connor put the card beside the bunk.

"He thinks I'm just coming home."

"You are. Eventually."

"Five years maybe."

The number felt more real now that court conversations were moving toward plea and sentence rather than pure imagination.

Stuart looked at him.

"Five years is not the same as forever."

"Easy for you to say. You think you're getting out."

The sentence came out sharper than Connor intended.

Stuart went quiet.

Connor immediately regretted it.

"Sorry."

Stuart nodded.

"It is all right."

"No, it ain't."

Connor looked at the card.

"I'm just..."

"Afraid."

Connor sighed.

"You love that word."

"Because you avoid it professionally."

Connor laughed despite himself.

"Five years still feels better than fifteen. That's messed up, innit?"

"Relief can be strange."

Connor nodded.

The little brother in the drawing did not care about sentencing maths.

He only cared about when.

---

## JAMO

Jamo received no cards that day.

He told himself post was post.

Could be tomorrow.

Could be delayed.

Could be anything.

He still checked twice.

Connor noticed and did not mention it.

That was another version of friendship they had learned.

Later Connor pushed half a packet of biscuits toward him without explanation.

Jamo took one.

"What are these for?"

"Christmas charity."

"Fuck off."

Connor smiled.

Jamo ate the biscuit.

Neither mentioned cards.

---

## TYLER

Tyler's mum sent a card containing a paragraph longer than most legal letters.

It told him she loved him, was angry with him, believed he could do better, had washed some clothes for when he came home despite not knowing when that would be, and reminded him to brush his teeth.

Tyler laughed out loud at the last line.

Josh looked over.

"What?"

Tyler handed him the card.

Josh read the bottom.

"Your mum's right."

"About what?"

"Teeth."

"Fuck off."

Josh grinned.

Tyler folded the card carefully and put it somewhere safe.

He did not need to pretend it meant less than it did.

That was new.

---

## MASON

Mason watched Christmas arrive through young men pretending they were not waiting for post.

He had seen it enough times to recognise the ritual.

The men with children became softer.

The men with nobody became louder or quieter depending on personality.

The men waiting for girlfriends inspected handwriting like forensic evidence.

Connor was doing that now.

Mason found him looking at the group card again.

"Problem?"

Connor looked up.

"No."

Mason nodded.

"Excellent. Love problems that look exactly like that."

Connor laughed.

"Her name's at the bottom."

"Tragic."

"You know what I mean."

"I do."

Mason sat nearby.

"What are you gonna do about it?"

Connor shrugged.

"Nothing."

"Good."

Connor stared at him.

"That's your advice?"

"You have information?"

"No."

"Then you've got a name written lower on a card than you'd like."

Connor smiled reluctantly.

Mason tapped his temple.

"Hear all. See all. Don't invent the bits you haven't heard or seen."

"That's not the saying."

"Expanded edition. Christmas special."

Connor laughed.

Mason got up.

Prison made imagination dangerous because there was so much empty space to feed it.

Connor was slowly learning not every silence needed filling.

It was one of the healthier things prison had taught him.

Mason found that depressing.
