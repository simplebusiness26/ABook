# Chapter Two — Mason

Mason could spot a first-timer before the first-timer had worked out where the toilets were.

There were signs.

Too much looking without wanting to be seen looking. Too much listening. Every buzzer treated like an announcement from God. Every officer's instruction received as though there might be a hidden second meaning.

The young lad in black had all of them.

He had been trying not to stare at Mason for the best part of five minutes.

Mason found this flattering.

He was standing beside the other reception orderly, arms folded, watching the lad attempt to divide his attention between the woman officer talking to him and the two prisoners he had clearly decided were murderers.

The officer clicked her fingers once.

"Connor. Look at me when I'm talking to you."

Connor snapped his eyes back towards her.

"I am."

"Then keep doing it."

"Yes, miss."

Mason smiled.

Connor saw him smiling and immediately looked even more worried.

That made Mason smile more.

He couldn't help it.

New lads always imagined the wrong things.

Connor probably had Mason down as a lifer. Maybe double figures. Maybe some terrifying old-school criminal who had been sitting in Lewes since smoking was allowed everywhere and televisions had backs on them.

The truth was less impressive.

Mason had simply been to prison too many times.

There was a difference.

He had spent enough of his adult life coming in and out of places like this that prison had stopped feeling strange before freedom had ever started feeling normal.

That was not something he was proud of.

It was simply true.

Outside, Mason forgot appointments, ignored letters and could somehow turn a perfectly manageable Tuesday into a disaster by lunchtime.

Inside, he knew where he was meant to be.

He knew which voice meant an officer was actually angry and which voice meant they were bored.

He knew the sound of a key turning for him and the sound of one turning for somebody else.

He knew when a new prisoner was pretending not to be frightened because he had done the same thing himself enough times to recognise the performance.

Connor was frightened.

Very frightened.

And trying desperately to make it look like concentration.

Mason had seen worse attempts.

Not many.

The woman officer finished laying down the law and pointed Connor towards the next stage of reception.

Connor moved when he was told.

He passed Mason close enough for Mason to see how young he actually was.

Twenty-one, maybe.

Twenty-two at a push.

There was still something unfinished about his face.

"You'll be all right, mate," Mason said quietly.

Connor looked at him as though this might be a trap.

Mason almost laughed.

"Cheers," Connor said.

Then he was taken through another door.

The door shut.

Mason turned to the other orderly.

"He thinks you're scary."

The man looked offended.

"Me?"

"Terrified of you."

"Didn't even look at me."

"Exactly."

The other orderly considered this and nodded, apparently satisfied.

Reception settled again.

It never stayed settled for long.

That was the strange thing about the place. Nothing happened for ages and then five things happened at once. Men arrived carrying the remains of whatever day they had just had outside. Court clothes. Tracksuits. Work boots. Sometimes no coat in winter because nobody had expected to end the day in custody.

Some talked too much.

Some didn't talk at all.

Some arrived furious at everybody except themselves.

Some asked the same question over and over because they disliked every version of the answer.

*When am I getting out?*

Mason had learned there were questions nobody in reception could answer but everybody asked anyway.

He was moving a stack of clear property bags when he heard an officer behind the desk say a surname.

Mason didn't react at first.

Names meant nothing in prison until somebody gave them meaning.

Then the officer said the first name.

"Craig."

The other orderly stopped what he was doing.

Not dramatically.

Just enough.

Mason looked across.

A man had been brought in through the inner door.

Mason knew the face, although he had never met him.

That was becoming normal these days. You knew people's faces from photographs passed around on phones, local news stories, court reports, social media posts made by people who claimed not to be involved in anything and somehow knew everybody who was.

Craig looked ordinary.

Mason had always found that disappointing about famous criminals.

The stories gave them height.

Rumour gave them shoulders wider than doors.

By the time you actually saw one, you half expected thunder.

Then some bloke in a tracksuit walked in and asked where he was supposed to put his property.

Craig stood at the desk while an officer dealt with paperwork.

He did not look angry.

He did not look nervous.

He did not look interested in the fact that three different men had now found reasons to glance in his direction.

The other orderly leaned towards Mason.

"Is that him?"

Mason kept his eyes on the bags.

"Looks like."

"Craig Craig?"

Mason looked at him.

"How many Craigs have you been expecting?"

"You know what I mean."

Mason did.

Everybody did.

Craig's name had been floating around Sussex for years.

Money stories.

Violence stories.

Stories about people who had crossed him and regretted it.

Stories about people who had stayed loyal to him and found that loyalty returned.

Stories about clubs, cars, houses, drugs, debts, favours and people in London who answered when he called.

Half of them were probably rubbish.

The problem was nobody knew which half.

Mason had heard Craig described as everything from a businessman who happened to know criminals to the man who quietly controlled half the serious drug trade along the coast.

Now there was talk of a conspiracy.

A big one.

Class A.

The sort of case where newspapers used words like *network* and *kingpin*, and everybody in a pub suddenly knew exactly how organised crime worked.

Mason did not know what was true.

That was another thing prison taught you eventually: certainty was usually just gossip that had been repeated enough times.

But he knew what reputation looked like when it entered a room.

It looked like this.

Men pretending not to notice.

Officers becoming slightly more precise.

An orderly suddenly taking twice as long to fold a plastic bag because it allowed him to remain nearby.

The room had not changed.

The people in it had.

Craig signed something.

An officer spoke to him.

Craig nodded once.

No performance.

Mason approved of that.

The loudest prisoners were rarely the ones worth listening to.

A man waiting by the wall whispered, "That's London Craig."

Someone beside him said, "Thought he was Sussex now."

"Same bloke, innit."

"What's he in for?"

"Conspiracy."

"What conspiracy?"

The first man shrugged.

"The big one."

Mason snorted.

There it was.

Prison intelligence at its finest.

Within ten minutes Craig would have been charged with seventeen offences, escaped twice and bought Spain.

By dinner somebody would swear his uncle was on the jury.

Mason carried the property bags across the room.

Craig glanced at him.

Mason gave the smallest nod.

Craig returned it.

That was all.

No introduction.

No need for one.

Mason had met men before who enjoyed being known.

You could see them waiting for recognition. They dropped names into conversations like loose change. They told stories where everybody important happened to owe them a favour.

Craig didn't seem interested.

That interested Mason more.

An officer directed Craig onwards.

As he walked through, one of the men waiting near the wall shifted subtly to give him more room than was necessary.

Craig did not acknowledge it.

The door closed behind him.

The room breathed again.

"Mad," the other orderly said.

"What's mad?"

"Him being here."

Mason picked up another bag.

"People come to prison. That's sort of the business model."

"You know what I mean."

Again, Mason did.

He just didn't like giving rumours more respect than they deserved.

A few minutes later another prisoner came through from the holding room.

"Craig's in there," he said.

The other orderly laughed.

"We know. We watched him go in."

"Nah, I mean everyone's clocked him."

Mason looked towards the closed door.

"Of course they have."

"Word'll be all over the place by tonight."

"It'll be all over the place before he gets his tea."

The prisoner grinned.

"Probably already is."

He wasn't wrong.

That was the thing about prison.

People imagined walls stopped information.

They didn't.

Information changed shape, that was all.

A name passed from one man to another. A phone call went outside and came back in somewhere else. Somebody knew somebody's cousin. Somebody had been in court with somebody. Somebody had heard something from a bloke who swore he had it from the source.

Truth travelled.

Lies travelled faster.

Reputation travelled fastest of all.

Mason had watched men arrive with nothing and build one.

He had watched men arrive with enormous ones and spend years trying to escape them.

He had watched young lads inherit reputations simply by standing beside the wrong person at the right moment.

That last thought made him think of Connor.

Mason looked towards the door again.

The young lad had gone into the same holding area as Craig.

Probably meant nothing.

It almost certainly meant nothing.

Still, Mason had been inside long enough to know that entire sentences sometimes changed because of things that meant nothing at first.

A seat chosen because it was empty.

A conversation started because somebody was bored.

A favour done without thinking.

A person you happened to stand beside when somebody else walked into a room.

He finished the job in front of him and, a little later, was sent through towards the holding area.

Connor was exactly where Mason expected him to be: sitting stiffly on the end of a bench, trying to look as though he had chosen that posture on purpose.

Craig sat opposite.

Several other prisoners were doing an impressive amount of not-looking at him.

Connor glanced up when Mason came in.

Recognition crossed his face.

Then relief.

Only a tiny bit, but Mason saw it.

That was another first-timer thing. One familiar face became family remarkably quickly.

"All right?" Mason asked.

Connor nodded.

"Yeah."

He was not all right.

Mason let him have the lie.

Connor's eyes moved towards Craig and back again.

He lowered his voice.

"Who's that?"

Mason looked at him.

"Craig."

"Yeah, I got that bit."

Mason smiled.

There was a bit more life in the lad now.

"What's everyone looking at him for?"

Mason considered the question.

There were dozens of possible answers and Connor did not need most of them on his first hour inside.

"Because people know his name."

"Why?"

"Because they do."

Connor frowned.

"Helpful."

"I'm not the information desk."

Connor looked towards Craig again.

Mason followed his eyes.

Craig was staring at the floor, apparently unaware that he was the most interesting thing in the room.

"Listen," Mason said quietly.

Connor looked back at him.

"First day in here, best thing you can do is not collect other people's problems. You've already got your own."

Connor's expression changed at that.

Only slightly.

Mason wondered what his problem was.

Everybody arrived carrying one.

Some carried several.

"I ain't collecting anything," Connor said.

"Good."

"I only asked who he was."

"That's how collecting starts."

Connor gave him a look that said he thought Mason was being dramatic.

Mason had given older prisoners the same look when he was young.

He remembered that too.

"You'll see," Mason said.

"See what?"

"Everything. Eventually. That's the problem with prison."

Connor looked confused.

Mason stood to leave.

Behind him somebody whispered Craig's name again.

Another prisoner answered with a story Mason had heard before, except this version was already bigger than the one he had heard outside.

Mason shook his head.

Craig had been inside Lewes less than an hour.

His reputation had beaten him there by years.
